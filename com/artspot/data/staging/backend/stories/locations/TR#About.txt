
Tripura, nestled in eastern India, is one of the smallest but geographically significant states amidst lush landscapes and dense forests. It shares an international border with Bangladesh's Tripurī division, fostering cross-border relations crucial for its socio-economic fabric.

Historically, Tripura was known as the Tripuri State, characterized by a distinct culture and languages influenced by Ahom rule until integration into India during British colonialism under Lord Wavell in 1947. The region later formally became Tripura state.

The population is predominantly tribal (Scheduled Tribes), constituting about half of those residing on hills and nearly one-fifth overall, reflecting deep indigenous roots across varied terrains including the Iravaddy valley plains. Assamese as an official language is significant in North Tripura, while the Gorkha descendants speak Nepali.

Tripura is often described as ecologically fragile but richly diverse. Its boundaries are flanked by Manipur and Mizoram on the north and Assam on another side. The capital city offers administrative significance against a backdrop of vibrant tribal culture throughout the state. Today, Tripura stands out for its constitutional history, previously recognized as one of India’s first Democratic Republic States before merging peacefully with India in 1949 (North, South, and East were merged to form modern Tripura).
