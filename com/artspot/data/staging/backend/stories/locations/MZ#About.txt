
Strategically located along the international border with Bangladesh and Burma (Myanmar), Mizoram is one of India's vibrant northeastern states. Situated between 21°N and 25°N latitude, it shares an approximately 368-kilometer length boundary with these two countries.

Known officially as *Mizoram* but often referred to by its indigenous name (which loosely translates to 'land of the gentle people'), this state gained prominence through successful agitation for full-fledged statehood in India on February 20, 1982. Unlike many other Northeast Indian states which were carved out from larger regions or princely kingdoms, Mizoram's emergence was unique.

With a population numbering around two million (as per recent data), the Mizo (or Cheraw) tribe forms the ethnic majority, constituting about seventy percent of the populace. The state is geographically characterized by hills and valleys stretching northeast-southwest from the Chin Hills across Patkai-Barah Valley towards Tripura.

Agriculture remains vital to Mizo/riland /Mizoram's economy, with tea cultivation flourishing in coastal areas such as Lawngirong and Chawlang. Historically unique among Northeast Indian states due to missionary-driven development efforts alongside significant tribal administration elements, this region stands distinct. Its language is closely derived from the Zara dialect of Manipuri, reflecting shared cultural links even at its borders.

Religion plays a crucial role in Mizoram society; most tribals practice Christianity through fellowship meetings organized annually as 'Mameta'. This blend of ecological beauty associated with dense forests and ethnic distinctiveness makes Mizo/riland /Mizoram a unique part of India, often compared favorably to other Northeastern destinations for its natural attractions.
