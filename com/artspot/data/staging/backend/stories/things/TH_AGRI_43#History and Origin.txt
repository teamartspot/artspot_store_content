
Bananas are among humanity’s most cultivated fruits, shaping global agriculture profoundly. Originally, bananas were wild berries with small stature, but centuries of selective breeding yielded elongated cultivars like the Cavendish (the common banana) or previously the Gros Michel, prized for their taste.

However, large-scale commercial planting led to vulnerability. A widespread fungal disease known as Panama Wart devastated many varieties across Asia and Africa beginning in 1950s attempts at expansion from resistant South/Central American cultivars. The TR1 fungus destroyed vast numbers of plantations using *Musa* species susceptible beyond the Andes.

The Red Dacca, likely originating near Darjeeling in northern India around a century ago (perhaps earlier in Bangladesh) was already immune to most devastating diseases including those similar to TR1 found elsewhere. Its distinct reddish skin colouring and characteristic flavour set it apart locally from other resistant cultivars that might otherwise become dominant here.

These red-skinned bananas often have different local names, including various Kamalapur Red Banana (KRB - but this specific label seems more recent than its existence; possibly representing a commercial branding or genetic refinement request), alongside others. While susceptible to *TR1*, they remain valuable.

Their cultivation likely expanded in the era of banana imports and plant movement restrictions from Europe around 20 years ago, possibly keeping farmers connected locally to immune landraces unaffected by widespread disease issues. This specific cultivar like Red Dacca or its Indian variants is a testament to traditional selection still active alongside global commodity movements.
