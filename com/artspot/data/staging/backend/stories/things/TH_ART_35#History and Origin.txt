
The history of Golu dolls traces back centuries ago to regions in Central and East India, particularly Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, Telangana, and parts of Madhya Pradesh. Their origin is somewhat enigmatic; representations might exist as early as 500 AD, perhaps derived from sculptures or figures used in temple worship over time. They are, however, definitely recognized as integral to the **Golu** tradition observed during major Hindu festivals like Diwali.

The name itself points towards their central role in devotional displays. During Deepavali (Diwali), these small clay figurines, often depicting deities from both Vaishnavism and Shaivism traditions – Lakshmi-Narayan, Navagraha kings (rulers of the night sky), various gods and goddesses including Ganesha himself, Nataraja, Palanesswaran, or Durga – are arranged in intricate tiers known as 'Gollus' or 'Golus'. This elaborate display is dedicated to these deities. The tradition likely evolved from displaying simple clay offerings at home altars or ancestral shrines during worship rituals ('pujas'), perhaps coinciding with pre-harvest celebrations around the autumn equinox.

These dolls differ significantly from contemporary commercial brands sharing a similar name, which draw inspiration generally but aren't identical artifacts. Historically, artists potters like **Kalamkari** artisans, known for their narrative depicting figures on intricate cloth scrolls called 'Pothana' or 'PaTina', likely pioneered these detailed clay images based on temple iconography.

The oldest surviving examples typically date from the 16th to mid-20th century. Aspects of this devotional arrangement practice also traveled to Southeast Asia via trade routes along ancient overland and maritime silk roads, influencing local festivals like Thailand's Songkran (Song Festival).
