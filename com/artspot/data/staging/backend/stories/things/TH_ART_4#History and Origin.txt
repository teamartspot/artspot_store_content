
Crafted under royal patronage during Mughal rule in India's coastal Karnataka region, Bidri art emerged as a distinctive form of blackened brassware around the mid-16th century (c. 1540 AD). Legend attributes its origins to a local artisan named Bukka alias Bidera from the village of Belavada near Shringeri, who accidentally created the characteristic dark metal through an experimental cooking mishap.

The core technique involves casting hollow vessels, primarily bowls and plates, in molds made from terracotta. These are then affixed with motifs created by repoussé – hammering thin butterscotch-gold sheet to form intricate raised designs against a deep black background that can crack beautifully over time. This decorative method was heavily influenced by the Persian art of Gol-e Now (Nineveh).

Emerging under Nayak and later Mysore Kingdom patronage, Bidri craft absorbed diverse influences from both native South Indian traditions and the syncretic culture fostered along the Western Ghats coastal belt. The distinctive 'Bidri Blue,' a hallmark gifting to this art form today derived from cobalt used in enameling the intricate motifs.

Though its usage declined with changing tastes over time, perhaps gaining popularity during European colonial period Bidri making comeback through Geographical Indicator recognition and continued patronage. Today it stands proudly as an acclaimed example of India's diverse artisan traditions.
