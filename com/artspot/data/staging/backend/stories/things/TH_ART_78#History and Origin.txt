
Maddalam is the name of both a region within Palakkad district, Karnataka, India, and a distinct form of folk dance-drama originating from there. Originating in ancient times likely around the early medieval period (roughly 12th to 15th centuries CE), it is intrinsically linked to the cultural traditions brought by North Indian traders and settlers into the Mysore Kingdom (later Wodeyar dynasty) region that included parts of present-day Palakkad. The name itself suggests a strong connection, likely deriving 'Maddalam' from Sanskrit words related to temple bells or musical instruments used in performance ('maddhu' meaning pleasant sound/drama and 'alam').

The core essence of this art form revolves around the worship of the Divine Mother (Devi), particularly Bhadrakali. Performances often depict the triumphs and adventures of Kalki, an avatar prophesied for Lord Shiva in Hindu scriptures to restore dharma at the end of a Kalpa or Yuga, as well as narratives from epics like Ramayana and Mahabharata focusing on heroism and valor. It is performed during village festivals (utsavas), harvest seasons ('kallola'), special days dedicated to Goddess Kalki Bhadrakali, known locally as 'Kalkidil Okai', and other significant temple events.

The traditional costume is the key identifier: performers wear long, brightly colored silk-like outfits called *Maddalu*, complete with large flowing sleeves extending past their elbows. Wigs are an essential part for the characters, adding to the visual grandeur of the folk spectacle dramas (kathas) performed by an all-male troup or individually. Originating in rural areas near Palakkad Fort and surrounding towns like Bantwal under rulership influenced by figures such as Tipu Sultan, its musical accompaniment typically includes chande played on veena and other string instruments ('kanjira'), tambura and pakhawaj for rhythmic support.
