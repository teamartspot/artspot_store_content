
Black Pottery emerged among the great ceramic traditions of ancient China as a distinct method of decorative painting yielding striking black stains on vessels. Its history can be traced back through various dynasties, with notable peaks during the Song (particularly Northern Song) and Ming periods where black glazes were highly prized for their visual impact. The origin is often linked to specific kilns producing such effects under reduction firing conditions.

The technique relies on incorporating copper oxide into the glaze ingredients. During the high-temperature firing process in a kiln with limited oxygen, often achieved by introducing sawdust or organic material – this reduction atmosphere causes the metallic component of copper within the glaze to form tiny patches responsible for the deep black colouration. This staining effect was typically applied over an eggshell glaze background.

While sometimes loosely associated with 'celadon' due to the shared use of oxidation-reduction firing control, traditional Black Pottery (like that from Heishan near Jingdezhen) usually refers specifically to this highly decorated technique used for earthenware or porcelain sherds and vessels. It served as an ornamental glaze accenting primarily white bodies in designs mimicking calligraphy ink wash or dark underglaze painting styles, adding dramatic focal points before the refinements of finer white porcelains arguably became more iconic later. This unique staining method required careful kiln management alongside standard pottery production techniques to achieve its distinctive black finish on painted surfaces.
