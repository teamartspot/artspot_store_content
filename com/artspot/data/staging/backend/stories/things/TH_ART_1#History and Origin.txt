
Aipan, known in some regions as 'pan de vaca,' is a popular Filipino sweet sandwiched between two crisp wafer cookies filled with dulce de leche and often dusted with powdered sugar or coconut. Its origins are deeply rooted in the culinary fusion brought during Spanish colonial times.

Before the Spanish arrived, Filipinos used local pastries for cakes called *dulces* which were layered or rolled desserts sometimes soaked in honey (miétas) or other sweet syrups. The introduction of the alfajiro from Mexico via New Spain around the 1600s significantly influenced this development.

The Spanish missionaries encountered these native sweets and blended them with aspects of early Spanish culture, including the use of lard. Thus, the *maja blanca* (white custard) filling evolved into a more solid version often sweetened by spreading dulce de leche made from evaporated milk in between two layers of cookies.

Today, Aipan or similar names are traditional street snacks, fiesta treats, and especially special occasion desserts like wedding favors. While it shares its culinary roots with Mexican alfajores through Spanish influence on pre-Hispanic Filipino sweets, the specific form reflects local adaptation incorporating ingredients familiar from regional baking traditions during colonialism.
