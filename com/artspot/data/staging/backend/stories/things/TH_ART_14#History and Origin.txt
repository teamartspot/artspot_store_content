
Color often dictates the fate of ceramic forms. Blue Pottery is no exception, captivating audiences worldwide with its deep, translucent blues reminiscent more of stained glass than typical mineral glazes. Its origins lie firmly in China around 600-700 AD (Tang Dynasty), where ancient artisans first utilized cobalt compounds to dye porcelain before high-fire glazing.

Initial attempts used copper for iridescent coloring popularized by Tang dynasty Persian influences or Islamic art, but these often faded or turned green. A breakthrough occurred much later than some sources suggest – the Ming and Qing dynasties (1368-1912) saw its refinement and widespread adoption. Cobalt eventually replaced copper as the preferred colorant for achieving lasting blue hues in translucent porcelain glazes.

The imperial kiln workshops were pioneers, developing techniques crucial to this medium's survival through firing. Chinese cobalt was distinct, offering a vibrant, reliable blue until European sources became available towards the 19th century. The craft then spread along trade routes, reaching Europe by at least the late Ming period via Portugal and beyond.

European porcelain quickly adopted the technique independently but often struggled to replicate the same depth found in China's finest examples of cobalt-decorated stoneware until they accessed authentic Chinese pigment or followed closely derived methods later. This exchange demonstrates a lasting aesthetic connection, showcasing how techniques traversed continents long before the internet.
