
Miraj, the resplendent city often called the 'City of Light', gifted Indian subcontinent a truly unique musical instrument: the **Miraj Sitar**.

This is not just any sitar; it's an instrument born within this specific, vibrant cultural heartland. Visually distinct from its Persian cousin and other regional variations, the Miraj sitar typically boasts far more strings – often six courses played by sympathetic resonance (ghiraan) alongside regular strings for melody and bass.

Its defining sound is pure Hindustani classical purity, marked by intricate tans (rhythmic improvisations), soaring melodic meend (slides) characteristic of gharanas like the Kirana or Maihar, and resonant keezars. The sitar's design reflects this: large resonance chambers, typically with a calabash body and flayed hide fretboard made from coconut shells, though other woods are also used.

The playing technique is demanding – musicians navigate its complex fingerwork on the untuned sympathetic strings while precisely plucking the main melody and drone strings. This unique construction challenges conventional sitar intonation; tuning requires expert craftsmanship to achieve the desired range of overtones for specific ragas (melodic modes).

Its primary role remains as a solo vehicle within Hindustani classical concerts, providing the devotional solokhsanok or rhythmic taans heard across much of India. The Miraj Sitar represents a pinnacle in its long lineage – an elegant symbol of Indian classical music's rich tonal variety and intricate demands.
