
Kavad Art, also known as Kadamba art or Kadova ware after the region of Srikakulam in Andhra Pradesh where it was found, represents a unique style of Indian polychrome pottery dating primarily between c. 10th and 12th century CE. While the pots themselves are medieval in origin, their distinctive red-orange glaze is referred to as 'Kavad' (meaning reddish clay or pot).

The tradition likely began much earlier, with its prototypes seen in ancient South Indian vessels from coastal Karnataka and even older examples found across the Deccan. The mature form emerged during the Kakatiya dynasty, flourishing under rulers like Ganapatideva who commissioned works at sites including Warangal.

Kadava ware pots are characteristically human-shaped or animal-headed tripods, intricately painted with a vibrant natural pigment on their buff earthenware base before firing. Often mistaken for Buddhist Gandhara art due to the occasional presence of narrative reliefs and stylized figures derived from earlier influences – including depictions possibly inspired by contemporary South Indian bronze statues that used similar poses – the carvings are predominantly Hindu, featuring motifs like Ushnisha topped heads with decorative knots or crescents.

The unique glazing technique applied as a final layer over these painted works provides Kadava art with its unmistakable identity. Its discovery and analysis in the early 1930s by archaeologists confirmed it as one of India's important indigenous contributions to terracotta art forms before colonization.
