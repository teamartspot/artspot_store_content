
Thanjavur paintings are a prominent art form hailing from Tanjore in Tamil Nadu, distinguished by their vibrant colors, rich gold leaf backgrounds, and strong narratives, often depicting Hindu deities alongside royal entourages or scenes from temple legends.

Originating during the Nayak dynasty (1529-1730), these paintings evolved into a unique regional style under the influence of prominent rulers like Chinnanamakkal Thirumaligaiyar and later masters. The background, known for its characteristic "kalam" paste made from powdered leaves or sticks (traditionally black jack, blue ashoka, or wood-apple trees), provides depth while the thick gold leaf creates a luminous effect under candlelight.

Compositions are typically horizontal scrolls focusing on religious themes, with detailed attention given to architecture and costumes, often drawing inspiration from temple gopurams. Characteristic elements include richly colored murals, intricate bead work sometimes painted as decorative borders (known locally as 'Kolattu Kalam'), stylized depictions of flowers adorning the eyes of deities – especially large lotuses for Lakshmi and small jasmine buds for Shiva.

Distinct from other Dravidian art forms like Ajrond, Thanjavur lacks floral motifs or portraits centered on Muslim figures (Miraculous Mandalam), instead being firmly rooted in temple-centric Hindu culture. While originally created in the palaces of Tanjore's royal families who patronized many such paintings for temples and palaces, they are now widely practised.

These visually striking works from a dynamic historical period continue to be highly valued cultural treasures of South India.
