
Terracotta, a material as old as human civilization itself, found a unique and vibrant expression in the hilly region of what is now Bankura district, West Bengal. The roots can be traced back to early settlements around ancient cities like Nalanda and Rajgir in present-day Bihar. Centuries passed before it truly blossomed locally.

Around the turn of the 19th century (c. 1806-1832), during the British colonial period, the craft took a definite shape. It gained prominence as miniature decorative pots used for temple offerings and festivals began to be produced on a large scale in five specific villages: Patharpara, Deogarh, Gobindghat, Niali, and Jatinara - the very name 'Bankura Panchmura' (Pancha meaning five) paying homage to these pith-hiding places.

Initially, artisans created small decorative items – flower vases, pen holders. But gradually, distinct forms of worship offering sets evolved, becoming increasingly artistic. The transition from simple utility pots to exquisitely detailed ritualistic sets marked the craft's maturity and uniqueness during this period (let's say mid-1800s onwards).

Bankura terracotta is recognized by its characteristic reddish-orange hue derived from local red clay, intricate hand-sculpted detailing, often featuring floral patterns and religious symbols like elephants heads or lotus motifs. While the craft developed in response to colonial demand for novelty items, its essence lies deep within Bengal's artistic traditions of devotional pottery. Today, it survives not just as museum pieces but continues to be practised and admired by many families still living near these five villages.
