
Kalighat Paintings emerged in the late 18th century from the Devasherastreeya art tradition flourishing near Dhamtari, Chhattisgarh. Initially crafted near temples associated with their patrons – likely belonging to the Kalilinga or Kalisoda communities active around Kalighat itself, after which they are named, and initially a form of devotional art.

Originally known for temple murals and paintings adorning stone lamps (kalai parna), these community craftspeople created intricate designs. However, their artistic legacy is more famous from the mid-20th century onwards due to preservation efforts by institutions like Delhi's National Museum following artists’ migration during land reforms in the 1950s.

The name 'Kalighat', meaning 'temple shade' or canopy covering Kalighat village ruins (perhaps destroyed in an earthquake), reflects their likely origin. These paintings depicted Hindu mythology initially, but quickly moved beyond religious themes to become popular narrative art reflecting village life and contemporary concerns – gods alongside landlords squandering resources, rural scenes, village festivals.

They often carried a sharp social commentary from the start, critiquing landlords or depicting everyday struggles with wit and realism over time. Kalighat artists were known for their itinerant nature, moving around villages offering services near temple gatherings, making their art widely accessible beyond high-brow court traditions found elsewhere in Orissa.

Today, Kalighat Paintings are a vibrant part of South Indian Folk Art Collections globally. Their transformation from primarily devotional works to populist social narratives offers a rich perspective on evolving artistic expression tied closely to popular culture and changing societal dynamics.
