
The story of Nagpur oranges begins in the 19th century during British rule in India. Father Lacarrière from France is credited with introducing true Washington Navel orange seeds around 1832, likely through a gift or exchange.

Recognizing their potential, especially for export as they were seedless and sweet, Parsik farmers of Nagpur took these nascent trees under their care. The region provided ideal conditions – rich soil transformed by basalt rock and sufficient rainfall from the west monsoon in November-December, perfect for ripening during harvest time.

Through decades of intensive cultivation and careful development, the Parsiks perfected this variety on orchard lands traditionally used for kinnow, aurat ka phool (Kinnow's parent), and sweet orange – *Moong* or Barhi. The resulting Nagpur Navel Orange became famous for its robust tree vigor even in challenging climates, large uniform fruit size, easy peel, seedlessness guaranteed during peak export season, and exceptionally juicy, sweet flesh.

Post-independence, the family orchards expanded, but much of today's productivity comes from land once dedicated to kinnow. The development of a world-class navel orange industry on these lands represents self-reliance achieved through adoption and adaptation centuries after its initial introduction by Father Lacarrière.
