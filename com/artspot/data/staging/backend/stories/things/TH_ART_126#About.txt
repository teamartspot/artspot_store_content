
Firozabad glass stands as a unique artifact from India's pre-Islamic historical past. Discovered in large quantities at Firozpore village near Dholera in Gujarat around 2019 (though its origins might stretch further), this material dates back to the early centuries of the Common Era, primarily associated with sites like the Hinju ki Pavari complex and radiocarbon-dated deposits within structures dating from the second century BCE. This suggests a significant history spanning roughly two millennia before Islamic rule consolidated in North India.

The most striking characteristic of Firozabad glass is its vibrant colouring. Unlike typical vessel glass or traditional window glass found elsewhere which often requires cobalt for blue hues, Firozabad glass achieves its rich array of greens and blues through naturally occurring uranium within the raw materials used to create it. Cobalt was detected in only about six percent of tested samples, making uranium the dominant colour enhancer. This distinctive feature sets it apart from much earlier Iron Age glass found across India.

Due to lead content (estimated between one-eighth to a quarter), this glass is classified as 'lead-barrium glass'. While this type faded with Islamic administration in Gujarat and surrounding areas by roughly 1000 AD, Firozabad exemplifies an important stage before the widespread adoption of soda-lime glass depleted of heavy metal additives. Its discovery near an old craft town associated with Ajanta Ellora poses interesting questions about its context; was it a decorative item used before disappearing locally? Or perhaps evidence of earlier, more extensive glassware production that could have been traded across India and as far east as Southeast Asia during the centuries prior to Islam.
