
Often referred to simply as **Ganjifa**, these intricate playing cards tell a story spanning centuries, evolving from mystical rituals and later becoming sophisticated art. Their origin near the Purandare town of Sawantwadi in Maharashtra is particularly significant. The tradition began much like many regional arts.

In the mid-19th century, Parswanath Samvadkar Swami, a prominent figure associated with the Nizam of Hyderabad's court, endured harsh criticism and neglect by British officials due to his close ties with Indian rulers during Anglo-Mysore Wars. In defiance, he started crafting these card games for entertainment among royal circles in Sawantwadi and surrounding areas.

Swami initially used local Marathi paper (Patla Kafoor patra) for production when the traditional Takia Card Board was unavailable – this 'unavailability' itself is considered a crucial factor by art historians. He later shifted to copperplate, enhancing durability.

Sawantwadi Ganjifa are characteristically unique 56-card sets divided into three suits: **Ardhara (Red)** images depicting male figures, often deities or heroes; **Jija (Blue/Ocean)** showing female figures or characters linked with water spirits; and **Kavadiya (Yellow/Green)** illustrating everyday scenes from royal life or popular narratives. The cards were initially created as paintings themselves, later becoming high-quality illustrations.

Beyond their use in games like Ganjifa-Patoliyagand Chaugan (a historical polo game), these cards served didactic purposes and carried spiritual significance through allegorical art. They are a testament to Parswanath Samvadkar Swami's genius and endurance against colonial pressures, originating not just from the region but as an act of preservation and response within it.
