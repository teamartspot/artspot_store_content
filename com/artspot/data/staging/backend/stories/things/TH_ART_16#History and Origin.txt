
Kashmiri miniature painting boasts regional styles, one of the most historically significant being Basohli art, named after the region around present-day Anantnag district, originally known as Dus. Originating roughly between 1605-1610 CE and flourishing during the reigns of emperors like Jahandar Shah in nearby princely states throughout much of the 17th century until its decline by the late 17th or early 18th century, Basohli developed under a unique mix of Rajput, regional Kashmiri, Persianate, and increasingly Islamic sensibilities after Mughal rule shifted away.

Unlike other styles emphasizing Sanskrit themes, Radha-Krishna narratives dominate much of Basohli's corpus. Figures like Shiva as Nataraja (the Cosmic Dancer), Garlanding Deities or portraying Panna Dasis fetching water from the Ganges are characteristic compositions reflecting its diverse inspirations. The distinctiveness often lies not just in subject but also in language; works frequently employ a unique metaphoric Basohli script derived from Kashmiri Pandit Sanskrit, adding another layer beyond mere iconography.

Though drawing technical inspiration and compositional frameworks primarily from Mughal masters like Abul Hasan and contemporary Rajasthani styles known as Rajoli, Basohli developed into its own recognizable style. The influence of Islamicate aesthetics combined with indigenous themes created a potent visual expression that significantly shaped regional artistic history despite the shift in patronage across North India towards other styles later on.
