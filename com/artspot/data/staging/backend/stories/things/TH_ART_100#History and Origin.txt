
Originating from villages around the Narmada river, particularly those inhabited by the Mina and Jarsani communities near Sanganer town in Rajasthan's Bundi division (earlier than often thought), block printing's sophisticated form, especially the famous blue-on-white style, flourished significantly after the British East India Company promoted cotton textiles in the region. This led to workshops proliferating between approximately 1850 and 1930. The Sanganeri prints were highly sought after for both export and domestic markets due to their striking combination of large areas of resist-dyed white background, often creating intricate geometric borders or shapes like palaces against panels in vibrant colours—predominantly cobalt blue, deep reds using madder root, rich yellows from turmeric, and earthy tones.

Utilizing a unique wood block technique where multiple blocks are assembled horizontally to form the design on one side of the fabric, Sanganeri printers achieved bold compositions distinct from other Indian textiles. The intense colour saturation becomes visually mesmerizing once separated into its component colours: blue (cobalt), red (raktaji or pichakai), and sometimes other dyes applied sequentially onto a pre-printed block-covered cloth piece.

Today, safeguarding the intricate resist techniques is paramount for this UNESCO-recognized intangible heritage craft. Despite industrialization threatening some traditional aspects, communities like Dabra-Bari in Bundi still meticulously maintain these ancient skills, preserving Sanganeri hand block printing's unique artistic legacy against fading over time and distance from its source.
