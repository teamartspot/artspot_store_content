
The Jodhpur Bandhej originated in central India during around 800 AD as part of the broader Ajrani Bandhej tradition, found across both Gujarat and Rajasthan. Its roots likely lie near Ajrar village west of Pushkar lake, from which its name *perhaps* derived.

This resist-dyeing technique reached royal courts by the late 16th century (during the reigns of Raja Man Singh and later Maharaja Sawai Jodha Singh in Jodhpur). Skilled weavers were patronized here, leading to unique developments suited for Rajput royalty. The process involves folding or twisting fabric using tie-resists like wax (*jhama*), cones ("resist pastes" as sometimes simplistically described, though more complex dyes exist too), and knots before dyeing with natural colours – often indigo blue on rich earthy red backgrounds derived from mango wood (*Baghar chhind*, a traditional local term). Sometimes intricate single-colour stripes appear.

Bandhej's prominence coincided with the arrival of Persian weavers across the border under Emperor Jahangir. These artisans created designs for the Mughal court, showcasing its incredible detail and chromatic richness before wider weaving production began in Rajputana workshops around the same time using imported raw silk (*Banihal silk*, also a Jodhpur term). While other regions experienced decline over centuries due to competition from indigo-dyed *kirpanvaam* fabric or machine-made textiles, efforts towards its conservation and revival are now underway through various NGOs and government schemes.
