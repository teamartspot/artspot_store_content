
Tanjore paintings, also known as Nayak or Chettiar painting, trace their origins to the vibrant court culture of ancient South India. Flourishing between the 16th and late 20th centuries under various dynasties like the Vijayanagar, Madurai Nayaks, and Pandya kings in the Tanjore region, their roots lie much deeper.

The earliest forms are found in temple murals from the Chola period (850 - 1300 CE) where themes were heavily inspired by Hindu mythology. The grandeur of later dynasties' rulers saw a flowering of royal art patronage, extending beyond religious depictions to include court scenes and portraits.

When British officials arrived in Tanjore for service, some highly skilled artisans like Chidambaranathan served alongside them. These defectors, along with their Hindu counterparts employed by figures such as collector Thomas Harrison, brought the unique style into commercial prominence through firms like Harrison & Co. The paintings began circulating widely across India and internationally.

Tanjore painting is characterized by rich, vibrant colors (chiefly gold leaf on black grounds), intricate gilding, exaggerated expressions in dioramas ('koodal) or panel art for idols, deities, processions, animals, birds, and human figures. Initially a form of idol decoration within temples and palaces, it became an independent commercial art medium valued globally.

Today, Thanjavur paintings are recognized by UNESCO as part of India's intangible cultural heritage, preserving centuries-old techniques born from the unique artistic traditions of Tanjore's court and region.
