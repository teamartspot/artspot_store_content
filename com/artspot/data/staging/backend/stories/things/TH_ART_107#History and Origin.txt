
Phulkari, meaning 'flower embroidery' in Punjabi, is a distinctive and vibrant style of embroidered cloth originating from Northern India and Pakistan, particularly associated with Punjab. Its unique origins trace back to the 18th century during the reign of Emperor Aurangzeb's rule over the Mughal province of Hindustan (roughly corresponding to present-day Punjab). The story is linked to the beloved wife of a Sufi saint known as Mīr Dard.

According to legend, her husband was taken away by an official sent by the King. Overcome with grief, she embroidered their garden rose onto his robe's collar using bright golden thread before sending him away. He later returned clothed in white but brought back colourful silks woven elsewhere that depicted Phulkari motifs under different names – 'Jangi' (cloth of war), often made from wool and silk for shawls and saris, and embroidered fabrics called *Chikankara*. The Punjabi tradition flourished significantly during the Majestic Age following Maharaja Ranjit Singh's consolidation of power. Women developed elaborate ways to pass on their intricate designs orally while working or tending the fields – through shared patterns and story-telling.

Men initially created Phulkari patterns, often drawing inspiration from floral motifs found in nature (specifically flowers), adding a bold, decorative touch typically in gold and bright reds. Over time, women adopted this for richer fabrics like *Shal* silk (*Phulkari da Kalanjiya*) to create luxurious clothing items or bedspreads called *Boli*. This evolution blurred gender lines completely, solidifying Phulkari's place not just as an inspiration but a developed art form cherished across the region.
