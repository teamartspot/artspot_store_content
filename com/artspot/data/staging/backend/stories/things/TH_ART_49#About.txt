
The intricate artistry of Khovar, originating from the northeastern districts around Rohtak and Ambala (formerly Meerut region) in present-day Haryana, India, stands as a testament to Northern Indian craftsmanship. Though less prominent than some other regional arts, its detailed work is significant and unique.

Typically associated with bronze or copper vessels, Khovar art transforms these materials into stunning, decorative pieces for religious use. Items like small flower pots, water bowls (kundis), incense holders (dhoopadans or lata ka kuan), lamps, and ceremonial trays are expertly crafted using a lost-wax technique, resulting in thick, elegant shapes often decorated on the exterior.

The aesthetic is instantly recognizable: complex, flowing designs dominated by floral motifs derived from local flora like jasmine and roses. These intricate patterns are often intertwined with animal figures, particularly elephants and peacocks, or symbolic geometric shapes executed with remarkable precision and a characteristic warm, rich palette usually featuring reds, yellows, golds, greens, blues (often sapphire), and grey backgrounds.

While primarily known for metal work, Khovar craftspeople also excel in terracotta pottery. These pieces are valued not only for their artistic merit but deeply connect to local rituals and daily religious practices involving the home deities ("lata"). Though overshadowed by more famous Indian arts globally, Khovar's resilient craft tradition continues locally, embodying a unique blend of religious devotion and sophisticated metalwork or pottery design.
