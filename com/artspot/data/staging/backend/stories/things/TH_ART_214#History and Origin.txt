
The tabla we know today traces its roots back to ancient percussion instruments called pakhawaj, which were integral parts of temple orchestras (Natak) in the northern Indian subcontinent.

Until around the 18th century during India’s Golden Age, larger drums like the dhamar pakhawaj or biraha pakhawaj were played on horse-rawn carriages. A complex playing method called *thal* (or thabal), which allowed notes to be drawn out of double strokes, was developed much later in Varanasi.

In Varanasi, particularly during the early colonial era with British rule under William Carey at Bethune Sans Souci School near the River Ganga's ghats, musicians began experimenting and transitioning away from these larger drums towards smaller ones, driven by musical innovations. This led to the rise of percussion virtuoso Parmanand Sharma.

Sharma refined the playing style, contributing significantly to *keerthan* or rhythmic storytelling through intricate bols (syllables forming the rhythm). He championed the lighter piraw ("head" drum) – often a single goatskin head on a clay body. Alongside this was developed the new khanjira, replacing the heavily built jhal.

The name "Banaras Tabla" or simply *Tabla* (*talab banarsi gharana*) became established as it clearly distinguished itself from other contemporary small tablas and newer instruments like the sitar accompanying the tabla. This association signified not just a type of drum but also the advanced playing technique developed around Varanasi's gharanas, solidifying its legacy as one of Hindustani music’s most important innovations.
