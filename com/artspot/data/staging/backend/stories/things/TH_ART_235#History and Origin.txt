
The history and origin of Tharu Embroidery are intertwined with the rich textile heritage of Uttar Pradesh's Terai region, straddling the plains near Nepal and Bangladesh. It doesn't have one singular founder but evolved among indigenous communities known as 'Tharus' or 'Thaarus'. The roots likely lie in utilitarian embroidered garments associated with Ayurvedic medicine bundles ('Thare') centuries ago. Early examples can be traced back to interactions during various reigns, including the Mughal period (c. 16th-18th century), who influenced court and decorative arts, though the distinct Tharu style crystallized later.

Historically associated with women's attire – notably dupattas ('choliya') in hues of dark green for Banaras and saffron/red/orange for Mirzapur areas – its designs became symbolic during British rule. When India banned similar textiles from its neighbours, it led to a flourishing as native artisans turned inwards, creating increasingly complex pieces with intricate metallic thread work (zari), fine silk or cotton stitches ('kalam'), mirror tiling ('meena'), and beadwork. These items were considered part of the family dowry ('Tarul'). The craft faced decline due to changing fashions but experienced a significant revival in recent years, particularly through Padam Kumari Devi Tharu's pioneering efforts teaching young women across districts like Chitrkoot, Mirzapur, and Balrampur. Her dedication earned her international acclaim, including Delhi's first Geeta Sethi award for promoting indigenous craft. Dubbed 'Saffron work' due to the prevalent colour sometimes echoing regional cultural symbols or ties to Himalayan landscapes, Tharu Embroidery today represents a vibrant continuation of these communities' ancestral artistry and their unique contribution to UP's diverse folk decorative traditions.
