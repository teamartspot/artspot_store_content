
Pokkali rice holds a distinct place not just in culinary tradition but also in agricultural innovation along Kerala's coastline. Known for its resilient nature and subtle saline tang when cooked, this rice isn't cultivated everywhere due to these very characteristics.

Its story begins with necessity; saltwater intrusion from the Arabian Sea made coastal lands notoriously barren and salinity-laden almost a century ago. Farmers struggled under the sodden conditions provided by poorly drained wetlands. This changed in the early 1960s through community-leveled intervention, specifically tied to two government projects: establishing rice research stations near Alappuzha (Alleppey) and utilizing low-lying lands reclaimed as part of coastal irrigation works.

These initiatives focused on discovering rice varieties tolerant – even welcoming – high saline content in water. Pokkali emerged from this dedicated effort. Indigenous cultivation by communities here, however, is ancient, predating formal research. They intuitively grew saline-adapted strains for sustenance, but the *research* that defined and popularized it occurred at these new experimental centers.

Plant breeders identified the unique Pokkali genes responsible for salt tolerance after observing performance under varying conditions near Kalpetta in 1963/4, though its foundation was built upon existing community knowledge. Today, while other rice types might find more fertile ground easier to farm commercially on some large-scale bases, Pokkali remains a crucial variety, especially integral to the unique terroir of Malabar coastal districts like Kannur (Mangaluru), Dakshina Kannada, and Uttara Kannada where its distinct saline flavour profile is prized.
