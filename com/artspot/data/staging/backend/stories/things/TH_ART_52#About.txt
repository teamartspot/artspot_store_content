
Lippan Art, originating from Maharashtra’s Konkan region and parts of northern Karnataka but perhaps most famously associated today through its unique use of mirrors reflecting scenes done in *Mud*. This folk art primarily involves sculpting intricate narratives on wet mud surfaces. The subject is depicted with remarkable detail – human figures mimicking each other's scale and size, often including animals like cows, bullocks, goats or sheep alongside characters involved in everyday activities set against water backdrops.

To capture these scenes visually for painting (now often done onto cloth), specific tools are employed: a sharp piece of broken mirror called 'bhad', thin wooden sticks known as 'thora' and 'barha', typically red sandstone powder, ash, or white slaked lime. The colors used in Lippan murals traditionally come from betel nut paste (red) and local leaves.

The distinctive technique mimics the clear water reflection onto objects placed strategically within the scene – perhaps a stick pointing across water where fish are shown swimming directly beneath it. This clever use of silvered mirror glass, reflecting back its image on the raw mud canvas gives the art its unique name and visual charm, making images appear as if glimpsed through an underwater viewport. The temporary nature of the initial mural against wet ground or sand underscores that what is represented must be captured swiftly by the artist to preserve it for posterity or display.
