
Ahmedabad Sodagari, also known as Chitraghanti or Kalamkatahi print from the Chitrakoot region near Delhi, represents a unique and ancient manual method of decorating fabrics. Originating likely in the medieval period with Mughal influence via Gujarat artisans traveling to Delhi for work around 1600 AD, this specific technique became prominent during British colonial rule.

Named for its distinctive three-stage process involving an alkaline solution mixed with salt (soda) – a blend creating unique shades – and applied under sunlight without water. In contrast to Ajrakh's use of waxed resist dyes needing multiple applications or Mysore's multicolored dying machine, Sodagari utilizes simple screen printing. The process involves using woodcut blocks made from fine-grained Teakwood (Shisham). Each intricate image is meticulously hand-drawn onto the block with a pointed stylus, then painstakingly carved out by hand.

After coloring the background and outlines on paper or cloth to reduce costs, sometimes just leaving a resist band before printing. The design for Sodagari often features mythological figures from Hindu epics like the Ramayana or Mahabharata, along with scenes of daily life, flowers, fruits, animals, geometric patterns (Kalamkole), and borders.

Woven fabrics, primarily silk and cotton but also rayon or linen blends, are used. They are passed through a vat containing this soda mixture multiple times on the blocks to achieve deeper shades efficiently before exposure. This art form holds significant cultural importance as items like pallus and borders were traditionally sent as offerings for prayers in village shrines dedicated to gods or goddesses known locally as 'Chudamini' and others, gaining recognition even at UNESCO level.
