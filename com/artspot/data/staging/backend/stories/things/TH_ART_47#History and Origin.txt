
Kasuti embroidery originates from the unique artistic traditions of the Kodava community in Karnataka, India, dating back centuries. Primarily associated with court attire during royal patronage eras like the Kingdoms of Coorg and Mysore, Kasuti transitioned beyond regional use to become a globally recognized art form.

Unlike many intricate Indian embroideris known more broadly as *Gota* work or similar border decorations, true Kasuti features meticulous geometric patterns rendered in gold threads threaded through silk fabric. The designs often incorporate symbolic motifs like stylized flowers (jasmines, roses), mythical animals (antelopes, peacocks), and figures of deities, arranged with stunning symmetry.

Its history is intertwined with Kodava women's status symbols. The complexity of the patterns reflected the embroider’s skill and social standing or dedication to her craft. While initially confined to royal costumes transforming bridal apparel became another key aspect.

Mastering Kasuti demanded immense patience due to its fine detail and high density requiring numerous threads per centimeter - significantly more than many other Indian embroidery styles like Chikankari or Shisha where the thread count might be considerably lower, making it heavier. This distinctive feature also explains how geometric patterns have remained a fundamental characteristic distinguishing Kasut from other forms.

In 2017, UNESCO inscribed the Basohli miniature painting style onto its Intangible Cultural Heritage list. Basohli paintings often feature intricate gold leaf work and vibrant colors, which share some similarities with decorative elements used elsewhere historically or contemporaneously within South Asia.
