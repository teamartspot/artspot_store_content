
Sohar paintings represent a unique form of folk art deeply rooted in Gujarat, India. Originating from the rural communities surrounding villages like Sohar and Palanpur, these intricate artworks often cover surfaces such as bamboo poles or cloth strips and scrolls with layers of vibrant earth colours.

The creation process itself is often primitive, relying on natural dyes derived from plants, minerals, roots, leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds, milk, and manure gathered locally. Artists use bowls for dyeing and twigs for drawing the fine patterns characteristic of this style. The primary tools are typically fingers or simple bamboo skewers ('chambars').

Colour symbolism is paramount in Sohar art, directly linked to agricultural cycles and natural elements. Black represents water sources like ponds or wells; red, soil fertility; yellow denotes sunlight and agriculture; green signifies vegetation; blue stands for rain clouds; and white symbolizes milk and the moon. These pigments are often sourced by visiting neighbours' homes or fields.

Meaningfully depicting scenes from rural life – farming activities, traditional festivals (including animal processions), village craftspeople (like potters preparing colours outside their home 'Dharampuri'), social customs, proverbs, agricultural cycles, and fertility rituals – Sohar art weaves together the collective history of the community. Each image serves as ritualistic storytelling itself. Aesthetically challenging modern sensibilities with what appears to be muddy washings but possessing a profound raw beauty capturing essential aspects of community identity through its enduring connection to nature's elements and cycles.
