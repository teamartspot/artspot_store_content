
Khovar Art emerged from humble, anonymous origins within the rural communities of Gujarat's Khovar region (formerly Kevadra). Its roots can be traced back roughly a century ago, born out of local folk traditions and simple techniques. Initially, these vibrant, portable paintings were created by village artisans for temporary religious purposes or private devotional use, adorning floors during festivals like Janmashtami or weddings.

The art form gained wider recognition when artists from the Kevad region sought entry-level work during periods controlled by Muslim royalty in Gujarat. This provided access to royal patronage and workshops at places like Mandore near Jodhpur (Jaipur). Exposure there, along with other regional influences such as Kishangarh and Bundi art, fostered its distinct identity.

Early Khovar works reflected this modest background but later absorbed the influence of Mughal miniature painting prevalent in India during that era. While retaining strong folk elements – bright colours often derived from natural sources like neem wood or spinach, organic compositions based on available materials, and thematic focus on simpler divine stories or daily life scenes – its form began to stabilize.

These early works were primarily for practical religious/ritual use rather than permanent decorative commissions sought by academies. Later developments established Khovar's unique style separate from the more prominent Mughal traditions seen elsewhere in Rajasthan, yet still displaying an appreciation for some aspects of Pahari painting through its distinctive portrayal and earthy palette.
