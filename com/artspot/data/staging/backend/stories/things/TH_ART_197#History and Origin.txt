
The Lanjia Saura, recognized as an Important Traditional Art of the Tribal Heritage (ITAT) now, is a distinctive form of tribal painting indigenous to the Saurashtra region of Odisha. Its deep roots are firmly planted in the ancient Varkala culture.

Originating around the early 19th century, this art primarily served religious and ritualistic purposes when performed along riverbanks. During significant temple festivals in villages like Lanjia Kondapi, artists would create sacred imagery on platforms made of split bamboo pieces (known as 'kottas' or 'palkes') filled with rice water pulp ('erumilla'). The name itself reflects its geographical starting point and ritualistic essence.

Key themes include deities from the village's own tradition, such as Nambi Malleswarama, Goddess Tripurasundhara, Gaja Lakshmi (Elephant Godess), and local figures like Kriti Shaligramas (female clan protectors) and Valabharatana (male clan protectors). Landmark motifs like the sun, moon, stars, parasol tree, lotus stalk with waterlily flowers, conch shell, elephant, snake, and various birds are prevalent, often incorporated as part of narrative compositions or to fill spaces rhythmically.

Lanjia Saura experienced a decline towards the mid-20th century but saw a remarkable resurgence through revitalization efforts in Odisha. It is now actively taught alongside other revived tribal art forms like Kalahasti Chitrakala, Konarka Patua, and Kavadham. However, artists remain vigilant about preserving its authenticity and avoiding appropriation by non-tradition bearers.
