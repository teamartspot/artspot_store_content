
Bidri is a distinctive Indian metal craft that evolved into prominence in South India, primarily Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Maharashtra, and Tamil Nadu. While sometimes distinguished as Nizami Bidri due to its strong Persian influence when flourishing under certain Deccan sultanates like the Qutb Shahi dynasty (particularly during Mir Osman Ali Shah's reign) from around the late 16th century onward, it is now widely recognized historically for its unique characteristics.

The name 'Bidri' itself points towards its origin near an ancient settlement known by that name in Andhra Pradesh. The hallmark of Bidri lies in intricate designs fashioned upon thin sheets of bronze or copper (often acquired via trade from places like Ahmedabad and Rajasthan), but uniquely identified by their finishing. These metals are combined with white Zincite crystals (ZnO, zinc oxide) during the forging process to impart a characteristic bluish-white hue when heated.

Skilled artisans shape this metal through techniques like repousse – forming sculpted reliefs on one side (the plate), then often burnishing it before painting over parts of the design in enamel for vibrant coloration. The remaining exposed areas retain their metallic sheen, usually nickel-plated silver or copper to enhance the contrast against the turquoise base. This results in visually striking patterns, deeply etched and detailed, frequently depicting flowers, animals, intricate geometrical designs, calligraphy on blue backgrounds.

Though its origins are debated, by the late 16th century, Bidri reached new artistic heights under rulers like Muzaffar Shah IV (Nizamudin Khan), who further patronized it and influenced later forms associated with Nizari culture. Today, Bidri art continues to be valued for its craftsmanship both within India and internationally, preserving a rich history embedded in its unique blue metalwork.
