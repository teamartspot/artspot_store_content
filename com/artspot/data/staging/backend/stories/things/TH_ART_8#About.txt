
A unique expression of Balochi cultural identity is Banni Embroidery. Originating predominantly in Pakistan's Balochistan province, this intricate hand-embroidered style often graces tents used during migratory festivals like **Banna** or the annual Urs ceremony honouring dargahs (Sufi shrines).

Utilizing vibrant red silk thread and gold zari against a dark base fabric – typically velvet for the ceremonial *banaa* tents, but also brocade sleeves or dupattas in homespun cotton – Banni Embroidery uses traditional cross-shaped ("khat khat") stitches. The motifs are diverse: animals ranging from dragons and peacocks (the tent's focal point) to wolves and leopards; fish including colourful "Mashriqi" patterns representing imported goods or wealth, alongside locally found species; geometric shapes and intricate floral designs.

Its deep roots run through centuries of Baloch artistry, symbolizing history, faith, and status. The skill requires long practice but doesn't necessitate complex tools. Mostly done with fingers for red ground work initially, followed by threads using bobbins or an* aari*. It is traditionally considered women's work, though men also contribute to its flourishing.

Exclusively Balochi in origin, Banni Embroidery is now recognized globally and even legally protected as a Geographical Indicator, representing the province’s unique artistic contribution.
