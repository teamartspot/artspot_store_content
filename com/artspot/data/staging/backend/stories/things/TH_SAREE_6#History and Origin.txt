
Kunbi sarees trace their origins to the traditional communities of Karnataka, particularly the Maravathars and Kumbis in regions like Mysore, Davanagere, and Coorg. Often identified as belonging to the Kunbis (farmers), these distinctive saris are a direct result of their way of life close to agricultural lands and natural water bodies.

Unlike many Indian saris with colourful woven prints or silk fabrics, traditional Kunbi sarees stand apart due to two key features: large vertical stripes against a solid colour base, and bottom hem cut like the end of a dhoti for comfort during work. Cotton is the primary fabric used.

The vibrant colours, often inspired by nature – from flames to fields – represent abundance, health, and prosperity. The bold motifs woven onto these stripes are authentic reflections of village life: bullock carts, musical instruments like the dholu ('tholu pavadaya'), farming implements, or scenes around village water tanks (kolaveri).

Weaving is traditionally passed down within families through generations, maintained by women alongside their household chores. Their connection with King Tipu Sultan of Mysore significantly shaped these unique saris.

Kunbi sarees were created not just for adornment but to embody the values and aspirations of rural communities in Karnataka during a specific historical period around the 16th century onwards.
