
**Bandhas Charchar Patthar Craft: The Enduring Art of Patterned Resists**

The craft known as **Bandha or Charchar Patthar** represents an intricate method of dyeing fabric from South Asia, particularly prevalent in India. Its essence lies not just in the finished pattern but also in establishing specific artistic authorship through named blocks and designated processes.

Charka Patthars, plural blocks (singular: charka), are engraved wooden blocks where designs are manually drawn or carved onto a flat surface using coloured resist threads tied in **bandhas**. The 'charchar' part historically referred to the meticulous labour involved – rubbing these knot-dyed bands firmly against the fabric during dyeing.

The history likely finds its roots much like many Indic block-printing techniques, predating modern machinery with which designs share visual similarities but distinct execution methods. These blocks are traditionally carved from mango wood (Altingia excelsa), valued for its density and ability to hold fine details through water-based dyes – natural dyes derived from plants like indigo, madder root, turmeric, or synthetic colours.

Originating in the Mysore region of South India among weavers' communities centuries ago, artisans learned to create these resist patterns before establishing distinct dyed block styles. The precise tying and placement (bandha) required deep understanding between designer, printer, and dyer, ensuring a cohesive result. This specific craft is typically linked to individual villages or regions renowned for producing distinctive charka blocks used primarily in hand-weaving processes.

Its enduring appeal lies within its capacity to produce elaborate narratives woven into fabric while relying on traditional materials, skills, and collaboration essential for any cultural artisan enterprise seeking recognition against evolving trends.
