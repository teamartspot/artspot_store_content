
Jain art finds its origins deep within modern India, evolving from the earliest principles disseminated by its preachers long before Islam arrived on the Indian subcontinent. It is rooted in ancient Jaina preachings that fostered a unique devotional culture.

The development process of Jain art became clearly defined during medieval times, flourishing primarily as rock-cut architecture and temple sculptures in Gujarat state between roughly AD 600 and the mid-12th century CE, following ascetic movements. These early forms heavily featured images representing Tirthankaras (the Jaina prophets) for worship or ceremonial purposes.

Later periods witnessed a continuation of this tradition under colonial rule ('Padams'). The religious text 'Kalpa Sūtra' provided invaluable descriptions and specifications that aided faithful reconstruction. Throughout these phases, Jain art significantly relied on patronage by powerful merchant guilds like the Nai, Mudra, and Banjaras at local shrines.

Common themes reflecting Jaina values dominate: narratives of mythology (often borrowing from Puranic elements), portraits emphasizing peace and non-attached movement, depictions of their cosmology. The central figure is always ethically derived from core doctrine – representing an exemplary person or ideal community member through artistic representation.

The origin traces back to India itself; the art form continues today at various Jaina religious institutions in both temple architecture (though differing significantly) and manuscript illustration contexts globally, especially among Indian diaspora communities.
