
For centuries, artists along India’s west coast developed a distinct style utilizing marble slabs overlaid with gold dust or paint – the precursor to Agate painting. While miniature book paintings employing similar techniques existed elsewhere in India earlier, this particular format is strongly associated with Gujarat and particularly its capital city, Baroda (formerly Vadodara), earning it the name "Agate of Cambay."

The history typically starts around the Mughal era's decline but solidified in popularity during British colonial rule. Maharaja Sayajiraj Gaekwad VIII of Baroda was a pivotal patron and innovator for this craft, promoting its creation under state patronage at his court beginning in earnest after 1936. He further refined it post-independence. Artists would carefully carve detailed scenes onto thin marble slabs, often replicating paintings by M.F. Husain or Raja Ravi Varma who were contemporary figures associated with the medium's revival and popularization.

A key aspect of Agates of Cambay is that these intricate images – depictions typically drawn from mythology and folklore themes like Sheshanand Parcheesi board characters featuring animals, divine beings, humans and kings – are encased within a precious marble material. The gold dusting enhances the vibrancy and creates dimensionality characteristic of this unique Baroda art form.
