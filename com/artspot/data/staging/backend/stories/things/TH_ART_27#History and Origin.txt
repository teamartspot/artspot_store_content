
Crewel embroidery, named for the specific type of coarse-woven wool fabric called crewel (perhaps originating on *l'herberge du Coudray*, though that's debated), emerged in Western Europe during the medieval period and flourished significantly throughout the 12th century. Its roots lie firmly in practicality rather than purely decorative needlework.

Initially linked to specific guilds, particularly wool weavers (fullers) but also tailors and merchants' guilds responsible for maintaining their ceremonial or clothing items, crewel was traditionally worked on linen canvas using heavy silk or later, wool yarns. The focus of this ancient craft was clearly functional, aimed at adding decorative elements like buttonholes, strengthening fabric by reinforcing stitches through the weave (stippling), and outlining patterns defined by underlying woven designs.

Where intricate thread work became hallmarks of wealth under the more elaborate Elizabethan fashions later on, crewel offered boldness. Its characteristic technique involved stitching dense pattern outlines with back stitches or stems, then adding textured dots within those lines using a split-stitch variation (the foundation for its modern stitch called Stem and Seed). Early works often used wool threads not just for the details but also as reinforcement threads due to their strength.

This strong-pointing style suited sturdy goods produced by the guilds themselves; it wasn't initially taught widely outside these communities, unlike the later rise of huckaback or counted-thread embroidery. Its origins are firmly anchored in practical textile embellishment during Europe's medieval era and well beyond into periods where wool was prized for its durability against coarse use.
