
Originating from the complex tapestry of South Indian history, the Bani Thani (Telugu: బాని థాని) are often believed to be descendants of communities practicing manual scavenging. The term "Ban" traditionally refers to waste or garbage, linking directly to their historical occupation which involved cleaning latrines – a practice considered ritually polluting in many South Asian cultures.

Their origins probably trace back generations before the 16th-17th century. Many local legends connect them vaguely with communities tied to temples (devakulam nayakas) or royal services, suggesting some foundational presence around that time frame for certain Banias/Thannis groups, but pinpointing exactly which sub-group or area within their later spread is difficult.

Historically settled in specific rural pockets across southern India – primarily Andhra Pradesh and Telangana on the east (like Nalgudi near Hyderabad), parts of Tamil Nadu (notably Tiruvarur district around Palampuram town), and isolated areas elsewhere, they were engaged in low-status occupations, including agriculture supporting this labour work, alongside their primary role. During British colonial times, practices surrounding untouchability persisted.

Today, many Bani Thani have largely transitioned away from manual scavenging or its associated menial works due to social reform movements and constitutional safeguards against discrimination. However, a significant portion still resides predominantly in scheduled areas within states like Andhra Pradesh/ Telangana/Tamil Nadu, often navigating economic challenges and striving for recognition beyond the stigma of their past classification despite efforts towards empowerment.
