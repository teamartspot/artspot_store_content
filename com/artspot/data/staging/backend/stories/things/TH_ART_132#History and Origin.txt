
Khurja Pottery originates from the ancient city of Kaushambi in present-day Uttar Pradesh, India, flourishing over centuries near Delhi and Varanasi. Its history deepens into antiquity, potentially linked to Buddhist art forms during Ashoka's reign or even preceding periods.

Dating back to the 12th century AD, Khurja became prominent under Islamic patronage. The distinctive characteristic lies in its creation of 'Sudama Ki' pottery – unique black vessels often adorned with intricate green patterns resembling embroidery (derived from specific glazing techniques). This signature look results from a specialized double-glaze firing process: the initial oxidizing phase gives vibrant green colours and opaque white, while a subsequent reducing atmosphere during cooling turns most surfaces deep black. The contrast creates visually appealing pieces.

Crafted traditionally by potters who are often women (known as Khurjiani Kaikars), they hand-carve patterns onto leather-draped pots using tools dipped in designs called *pajwanis*. Originating for functional, everyday use – like storage and utensils – the craft now blends with decorative arts. Made from local terracotta clay kilned at low temperatures (around 1050°C), Khurja pottery represents a unique fusion of indigenous materials and techniques inherited from centuries-old workshops. The tradition continues today as part of India's living heritage, admired globally for its distinctive glaze effects and craftsmanship.
