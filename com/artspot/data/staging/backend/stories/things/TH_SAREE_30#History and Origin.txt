
Patola sarees trace their origins back centuries to Bengaluru (formerly Dacca) in erstwhile East Pakistan. Deeply rooted in the cultural fabric of West Bengal, they found a significant patron among the aristocracy and weavers there.

The hallmark of Patola lies in its intricate *Benimaspattu* weave structure. A fully articulated cylinder loom creates a grid that allows threads to be raised selectively back into their bobbins for building complex block patterns before weaving or after completion, unlike typical reed-and-bobbin structures. This technique facilitated the high-density *zari* (metal thread embroidery) work.

They are named 'Patola' due to an ancient belief regarding the origin of this unique loom and its heavy *jali* (perforated net). Legend recounts weavers observing light pass through perforations in bamboo blinds on a palanquin carrying Persian princesses returning from Agra. Adapting this principle, they mounted warped threads on bobbins inserted into concave cylinders of seasoned calamus (*haldi udumbara*), the dried stems of *Cyperus helferi*. This structure ensured that each thread reached its designated position automatically from either side.

Beyond aesthetics, Patola sarees are deeply tied to spirituality. The Dwarawasa or Dwarpala motifs, often found across multiple panels bordering the sari, traditionally represented guardians welcoming deities during community *Durga Puja* rituals in Kolkata. This connection amplified their perceived sacredness and importance for temple use.

Today's Patola sarees are celebrated globally as an intangible cultural heritage by UNESCO due to their extraordinary craftsmanship, especially complex multicoloured *zari*, and cultural significance. The entire process from dyeing raw silk threads (often using mineral-based traditional dyes) to intricate zari work takes immense time—typically several months per yard of fabric—and supports invaluable artisanal skills.
