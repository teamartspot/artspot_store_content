
Thanjavur dolls are a renowned Indian folk art form originating from the city of Tiruchirappalli (commonly known as Thanjavur) in Tamil Nadu. Their history traces back to the late 19th century during the British Raj.

The novelty arose from figures exhibited by Parsi theatre troupes visiting Thanjavur for royal presentations. Seeing these figures, local artisans began creating similar-sized dolls replicating the characters' attire and poses to amuse royalty returning from performances – hence the specific dress motifs (saris with broad vertical stripes on black backgrounds) derived directly from Parsi costumes.

The primary material is locally sourced wood, preferred for its grain patterns suitable for carving. This characteristic mirrors temple carvings in their fine sculpting detail. Figures painted in vibrant colors are distinct too. Each figure has four unique traditional poses (Nattupola), depicting different stages of life or narrative scenes inspired by classical dance and storytelling.

Hand-painted on finely woven cloth, they were originally fashioned with just fabric bodies filled with cotton stuffing. A unique feature is their articulated arms controlled by strings through the palms – a complexity not present in other major Indian dolls like Puttalam.

While initially created for amusement related to Parsi theatre, today thanjavur dolls remain deeply connected to Tanjore (Thanjavur) Dravidian temple culture and traditions of yesteryears. They elegantly serve as miniature repositories, showcasing regional craftsmanship, history through dress motifs, and artistic aesthetics in a compact form.
