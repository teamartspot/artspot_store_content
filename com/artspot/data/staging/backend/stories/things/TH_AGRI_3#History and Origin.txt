
Saffron, botanically known as Crocus sativus, holds significant historical sway across many ancient civilizations, often associated with luxury, power, and even divine qualities. Its earliest cultivation records date back millennia in regions like Greece, Persia (Iran), and parts of Asia Minor. Yet, the profound spiritual significance embedded within its roots gives a special dimension to Kashmiri saffron.

Kashmir's origin story is particularly rich, intertwined with Buddhism since at least the 3rd century BCE before Islamic rule solidified centuries later. Local lore credits saints like Padmasambhava (Guru Rinpoche) and Lal Ded in spreading its cultivation during the reign of Kashmir's Hindu-Kingdoms' rulers, notably Zain-ul-Abidin. It quickly became integral to both spiritual ceremonies and aristocratic displays.

Himalayan soil, especially within the Zardos region ("golden meadows"), is legendary for favoring this delicate crocus. Here it flourishes, producing threads prized not only for their vibrant color but also for a unique viscosity when soaked – qualities mimicking ancient saffron descriptions. This distinctiveness led to specific traditional varieties.

Kashmiri saffron cultivation truly became established under Zain-ul-Abidin's reign (1420–1473), who promoted techniques like intercropping saffron bulbs with vegetables in winter to maximize land use for spring harvests. This region-focused growth ensured quality unique enough that Kashmir acquired the reputation of being a major source.

Until the 19th century, the finest saffron threads came from 'Kashmir', often simply labeled as such even when originating elsewhere globally and regionally (though Iran still generally leads total supply), underscoring its historical high regard. The association with spirituality coupled with exceptional quality cemented Kashmir's place in saffron history.
