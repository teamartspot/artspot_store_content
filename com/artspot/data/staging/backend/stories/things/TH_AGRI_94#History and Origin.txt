
Joha rice stands as one of Assam's most cherished indigenous paddy varieties, deeply woven into the cultural fabric. Its origin traces back to ancient times within Assam's unique, diverse geography characterized by varied soil types and climatic zones.

Known for its distinct aroma, shorter stature compared to many other local boro rice varieties, lower yield under modern intensive cultivation practices, but notable resilience in challenging conditions (like standing flood-prone areas), Joha is more than just a grain. It's symbolic, integral to the culinary identity of various indigenous communities – particularly the Ahom rulers and tea garden workers like Garos or Bodo.

The traditional method involved human intervention like ratooning – cutting mature stalks that still yield grains. Even during colonial times when authorities often restricted cultivation under forest laws as it was associated with swiddens (shifting agriculture) in forested areas for some period, locals' affinity and successful management of Joha ensured its survival.

Today, efforts focus on preservation rather than large-scale expansion, recognizing its historical significance and agricultural heritage. The distinctive aroma is a result of specific rice genes present only in this variety within the entire country. It remains one of Asia’s distinct aromatic short-grain rice types. Cultivated across diverse zones – from foothills to plains – Joha rice continues to hold pride of place among Assamese rice varieties, embodying local agricultural tradition and unique gastronomy.
