
Madhubani Paintings represent India's ancient art form originating from the Mithila region (now part of Bihar and Nepal), particularly from community life. Initially a ritual act, the art is traditionally created by village women on temporary surfaces like mud walls, tree trunks, cloth, or paper using natural colours derived from plants, flowers, seeds, turmeric, milk, etc., alongside fingers, twigs, bamboo sticks, and cloth strips.

These unique artworks depict vital aspects of everyday tribal society: nature's beauty (animals, birds, fish), flora and fauna, domestic settings, and the most important subject – divine figures associated with village goddess worship. There are no pre-defined compositions; artists intuitively create intricate patterns based on their interpretation of narratives drawn from nature, dreams, or personal visions.

The rich cultural heritage was famously preserved by Bishnu Dev Pandey, a renowned artist who documented these paintings and transferred the tradition to canvas, making it portable as art for sale. The term "Madhubani" translates literally as 'Honey-wooded land' (land of honey trees), signifying its intimate connection with these forest-bordered communities.

Distinctive characteristics include large-scale compositions without borders, vibrant colours often used in flat washes ('bijhu') broken by intricate dark lines ('kolishi'), and an underlying rhythmic flow that gives the art a dynamic quality. Despite their simple materials and initial functional purposes – serving as house decorations or ritualistic offerings – Madhubani paintings possess sophisticated artistic sensibilities valued globally today.
