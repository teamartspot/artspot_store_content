
Jute craft possesses a rich history deeply rooted in the fertile landscapes of Eastern India, particularly West Bengal. Primarily derived from jute, a long brown fiber obtained from the *Corchorus* plant, its origins trace back centuries through agricultural knowledge prevalent locally near major river systems like the Ganges basin.

Initially used for practical purposes such as weaving sturdy fishing nets and coarse bags for commodities, the craft demonstrated utility in producing strong textiles. Basketry forms were also fundamental, providing essential storage solutions for farmers. Over time, artisans developed techniques to create wall hangings and intricate designs, elevating handlooms into significant decorative arts.

The craft flourished significantly during British colonial rule (c19th-early 20th century). Jute emerged as a vital trade commodity globally, particularly in the booming textile industry of Manchester. This encouraged artisan proliferation across villages in West Bengal and other jute-growing regions along fertile deltas like Assam and Andhra Pradesh.

Post-independence, while demand shifted from packaging to textiles elsewhere, government promotion and cottage industries schemes helped sustain village craft traditions. However, Jute handicrafts face modern competition from cheaper machine-made fabrics and plastic alternatives. Its heritage remains intrinsically linked to eco-friendly, heavy-duty bags (Jute fabric) like sustainable tote bags.
