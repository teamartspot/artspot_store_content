
The intricate art of Rupa Tarakasi, also known as the Silver Filigree work of Cuttack (in Odisha), finds its roots deeply embedded in history and local lore. Its origins are often associated with the pre-Islamic period, mentioned in ancient texts like Silappathikaram via the 'pathas', an early form of using wires in goldsmithing.

Often linked to theatrical forms like Kathaputaḷa, where performers adorned their characters' masks and costumes with such ornamentation – referred to poetically as Jangandira pathinu (Jangandira’s necklace), or more romantically as Khandapani taranga (the neck or chain of the golden bird), silver was likely known before large-scale industry arrived.

Located strategically near a source of potash ash, Cuttack became an ideal hub. Artisans traditionally heated two copper rods together in fire until they fused and then dissolved the resulting alloy in water – an ancient technique called 'mithuna' translating roughly to 'coitus cordialis', symbolizing divine love or perhaps just technical perfection achieved through natural heat!

The term 'Tarakasi' itself refers to silver treated with a mixed oxidizing solution. While some distinguish it from similar crafts elsewhere, Cuttack’s Rupa Tarakasi flourishes in its unique name and execution called Mardala (fret art), involving crafting borders of fine metal tendrils on flat surfaces or stones before setting gems.

Today, Rupa Tarakasi stands as a distinct example among India's varied jewellery traditions, celebrated for its detailed wirework, often featuring geometric patterns like the lotus. Its presence, born perhaps in ancient dynasties like the Eastern Chalcolithic culture around 2000 years ago and sustained through historical trade routes via ports like Paradip nearby to supply materials globally, marks Cuttack's enduring legacy of fine craftsmanship. This traditional jewelry continues to represent centuries of refined artistic expression.
