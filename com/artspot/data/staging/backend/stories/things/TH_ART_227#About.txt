
Originating from the small village communities near the confluence of the Varuna river (Banaras) and dedicated centres like Ramnagar in Uttar Pradesh, India, this is an ancient art form deeply intertwined with tribal life and spirituality. Traditionally executed directly on freshly plastered wet walls using a paste mixed with Pipla *husk* or other edible seeds as adhesive and outline guides – dried naturally with sunlight onto clay backgrounds – the distinctive murals typically depict mythological figures from Hindu, Buddhist, and Tantric traditions, vibrant depictions of flora and fauna, everyday scenes reflecting tribal beliefs and practices, and often contemporary social commentaries addressing gender equality, environmental concerns, or conflict resolution. Natural colours derived from forest ingredients like turmeric (*haldi*), acacia leaves (kaya *pati* - now also substituted with synthetic dyes), stone soot, lime, oil from nuts, and ghee are traditionally used for filling the vibrant patterns.

Banaras Mural (Madhubani) Painting employs unique techniques rooted in village life. While often seen as distinct due to its large scale and detailed figures (including elephant-headed Ganesha), there is significant overlap with other forms of rural Indian narrative art, particularly Phad paintings from Madhya Pradesh. Today, Banaras murals transition between being practiced on actual community walls – creating a living environment story, sometimes even today still continues in village settings, though mostly for specific festivals or private commissions - and being recreated on canvas by traditional artisans to reach global audiences. Though some modern elements have crept in, often with the intention of incorporating western aesthetics into Indian art forms, this continuation serves as a powerful visual language about India's rich cultural heritage, societal values, harmony, tolerance, peace, and women empowerment, making Banaras murals a poignant message against conflict for modern times too.
