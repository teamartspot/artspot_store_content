
Bagh Prints hail from the Baghpuri region, now part of Etah district near Delhi but culturally and historically intertwined with Madhya Pradesh. Their name itself means *'fox town'* or associated with *'bagwa',* a forested area nearby renowned for its abundance of foxes.

Though materials and techniques are shared across states, it's the origin rooted in specific locations like Chanderi that defines them uniquely as Bagh Prints here. These intricate resist-printed fabrics represent the rich heritage of Bundelkhand. The art is distinguished by *bagwa printing* – a technique executed using blocks carved from wood.

Artisans are believed to have received inspiration during royal hunting expeditions, seeing patterns mirrored in forest scenes before them on their return journey home. Initially developed for royalty and aristocracy, the demanding craft was primarily patronized by kings, queens, courtiers, dharis (palanquin bearers), pahiyaars (bearers of palaces and elephants), musicians, menials involved in royal processions, and barbers, signifying status and region.

The characteristic feature lies in its creation via *block printing* rather than tie-dye or wax-resist techniques like Bandhani or Shibori. The elaborate peacock design adorning these fabrics from Bagh is indeed famous – perhaps the most iconic pattern globally for this specific print style originating here in Madhya Pradesh.

This tradition, practiced continuously since at least the 18th century and likely much longer, showcases India’s enduring appreciation for intricate textile artistry tied to particular geographic roots.
