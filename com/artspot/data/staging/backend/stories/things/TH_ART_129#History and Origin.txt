
Warli painting traces its roots to the communities surrounding Nashik and Shahapur in India's Maharashtra state, likely developing over centuries as part of informal village performances or rituals. Traditionally created by women elders for storytelling or religious occasions, these early artworks weren't primarily decorative.

The distinctive characteristic lies in its figures: highly minimalist stick-like shapes, often showing deities like Dattarashtra Peshwa alongside local earth gods such as Bhairavnath and Ambani Devi. These simple forms represent humans, animals (like hens), trees, villages, houses, and farm implements with remarkable economy of line.

Initially painted on mud walls using locally gathered chalk powder mixed traditionally for a white hue against brown field or wall surfaces, the art was adapted centuries ago to use lime-based paints applied permanently onto temple floors. Around 1967-68, Pallavi Nikam (formerly Javeri) played a pivotal role in its evolution and recognition as a formal art form.

She creatively made temporary installations more permanent by using commercial synthetic colours on large wooden boards or canvas sheets. This transformation turned the traditional rituals into marketable, easily transportable works of art. Her efforts have brought global attention to Warli painting since the early 1970s, but its artistic foundation remains deep in Maharashtra's folk traditions.
