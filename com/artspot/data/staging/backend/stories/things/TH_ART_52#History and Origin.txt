
Lippan art, alternatively known as Mud and Mirror work (Pathar ki Chitra), is an ancient Indian composite painting technique with roots potentially in the 14th century AD, traditionally found in the Mithila region of Bihar.

Unlike conventional paintings on cloth or paper that rely directly on pigments, Lippan artists worked indirectly. They created vibrant scenes initially by drawing with mud paste (Pathar ka mirch) mixed with natural substances onto a white base layer.

The unique feature lies in its use of glass mirrors (Dhakka). Once the initial layers and intricate details were formed using these brittle surfaces reflected against each other, artists would apply bright colours derived from mineral ores and plant extracts over specific sections. After drying and burnishing, mirrors were removed or broken away to reveal the mirror pieces reflecting the colourful patterns.

The name 'Lippan' itself seems connected to its materials – referring perhaps to 'Pathar ka Chitra', meaning picture of stone (mud), since mirrors are broken like glass ('chitr patra') for parts. The tradition is often linked to Goddess Lakshmi, symbolizing prosperity and fortune, likely due to the mirror's association with reflection, clarity, and beauty.

Primarily developed in Patna under Laxmananda Tripathi as a distinct school later, this art form involved layers of vibrant colours derived from mineral ores and plant dyes applied selectively over specific sections covered by broken glass mirrors or embellishments. A medium was drawn on the base layer using mud paste mixed with other natural ingredients.

Though rich in symbolism, particularly drawing parallels to ancient pharaonic paintings found in Peru, its distinctive use of reflective elements led to it fading relevance with the advent of more readily available paints and new materials, highlighting how innovation depended closely upon existing resources like mirrors 'available' locally.
