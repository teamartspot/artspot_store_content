
The Adilabad region (now part of Telangana) possesses a unique cultural feature through its Dokras. Originating during the Deccan Sultante period around the 13th-14th centuries AD, these Dokras are likely independent communities or small local chieftains existing within the territories ruled by entities like the Kakatiyas, then later influencing sultanates such as Golconda (Qutb Shahi) and Bijapur. Their name points to their subservient position under larger rulers – 'Dok' is often interpreted in various tribal contexts across India, generally meaning a tributary chief or follower, essentially indicating vassals.

As agricultural cultivators and auxiliaries (faqirs), they provided military service ('Diwan') for the sultanates in exchange for protection from land grants. This system placed them formally below the ruling powers but granted some recognition and rights within communities like that around Adilabad Fort. They formed a distinct community with their own language, traditions, rituals including temple services at sites like Kamakshi near Rushikonda (where they are known as Koykanur), and specific cultural ties within Nizamabad districts including Rama Raju mandals where their villages were historically part of Rayara village Panchayat system. Though evolving throughout the subsequent history with the rise of the Mughals and eventually the British colonial administration, their identity as a tribal community reliant on cultivation while functioning politically under dominant rulers remains central in areas like Adilabad today. Their survival speaks to adaptation through various ruling regimes.
