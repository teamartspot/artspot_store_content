
Banaras wood carving, originating from the ancient city of Varanasi in Uttar Pradesh, India, represents a rich tradition spanning centuries. Early influences stem from Buddhist art prevalent near Nalanda, around the 5th century CE, showcasing motifs like lotuses and deities on simpler wooden objects. However, its distinct form evolved significantly during the Mughal period's influence.

During Akbar's reign (late 16th century), Persian aesthetics merged with Indian craftsmanship via royal patronage at Fatehpur Sikri, which hosted workshops drawing talent from across regions including Banaras. This era refined relief carving techniques and detailed floral borders seen today. The British arrival in the mid-1800s led to its decline as block printing became more dominant due to faster production.

Despite this, the craft endured within Varanasi, likely hidden or practiced among local artisans for generations until it resurfaced post-independence with Indian heritage focus and tourism growth. There's a concerted effort now by NGOs and government schemes (like Prime Minister Kaushal Kshitij Yojana) to revive and sustain these traditional skills.

Banaras wood carving remains significant, celebrated globally, adorning mandapams in temples, household items like trays and boxes, festival paraphernalia, religious icons at places of worship locally known as mandirs, art galleries around the world, and even featured on UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage list for its profound value. Its enduring appeal lies in its intricate detail, deep-rooted history, and artistic expression unique to the sacred city.`
