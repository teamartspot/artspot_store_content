
The Allagadda Stone Carving serves as a tangible, albeit modest, link connecting us to ancient India's imperial past. Situated near the present-day city of Hyderabad in Telangana State, this unique engravings adorns an outcrop of red sandstone.

Dating primarily from the Saka period (roughly 3rd century BCE), although believed sculpted under the Rashtrakutas dynasty and thought to be rediscovered inscribed around the year 2016-2017 after remaining mostly buried or hidden for centuries, Allagadda provides invaluable evidence of historical developments. The stone reliefs often illustrate a mix of secular scenes like processions, assemblies (suggesting administration), military parades perhaps indicating army composition and naval power (Saka kings were noted ship-builders, chronicled by historians in works discussing India's maritime history). Alongside are depictions of elephants, mythical creatures, and traditional motifs.

The inscriptions at Allagadda, predominantly found on the faces carved into stone slabs forming elaborate patterns, are particularly noteworthy. They primarily reference land grants made to Brahmins and farmers (Kulacaras), specifying areas, sometimes even listing names. This contrasts somewhat with later royal inscriptions focusing heavily on themselves; instead, the carvings offer a glimpse into contemporary society structure at that time through images perhaps representing local festivals or community events organized under specific administration.

Its location makes Allagadda not just another find in Sannati but adds a layer to understanding cultural transitions across what is now Telangana. It showcases artistic skills and perhaps records ancient trade routes, adding depth to historical research.
