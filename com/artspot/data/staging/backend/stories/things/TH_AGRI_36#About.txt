
Christianity arrived in Assam through Dutch and German missionaries from 1654 onwards, much later than Saint Thomas Christians elsewhere. Today’s Orthodox community traces primarily its origins to these mid-to-late seventeenth-century arrivals who worked mainly in the Barpeta region.

These adherents of the East Syrian rite or *Jacobite* tradition – often referred to simply as Mar Thoma Syrians – have developed a unique identity, distinct from both other Protestant communities and the ancient Rite Hindu (Suri) Christians. Geographically concentrated in areas like Tura, Sonari, and parts of North Guwahati, they form one of Assam's significant Christian minorities.

Their faith is characterized by specific liturgical traditions originating from Antioch, practices distinct from those established by Latin or Anglican influences, and their church structure reflects the autonomous *Mar Thoma Syrian Church*. Despite a small numbers compared to other religious communities in historically strong Ahom state, their resilience during periods of displacement post-1947 speaks volumes. They have often been associated economically with regions like Singorgaon in Karbi Anglong, contributing quietly and persistently as an enduring fixture within the Assamese socio-cultural tapestry despite being vastly outnumbered by other religious denominations.
