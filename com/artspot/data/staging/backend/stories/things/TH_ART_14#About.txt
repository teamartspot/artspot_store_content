
Blue Pottery stands as a captivating form of ceramic craftsmanship, distinguished by its unique decorative motif created using coloured slips traced onto the un-dried white clay surface, firing until they blend into an integral, solid colour part of the final piece. Its enduring mark is the deep blue born from cobalt oxide pigments.
The technique originated in Persia (modern-day Iran), where artisans perfected pouring and shaping colourful glazes or engobes over pottery during its drying phase before baking it at high temperatures to fuse them permanently into the clay body itself, altering both colour and texture. Over time, Persian designs and techniques crossed cultures, notably finding expression through Mughal influence.
Gaining prominence was a journey that reached India's Golconda Fort in Hyderabad around 200 years ago. This Hyderabadi style rapidly evolved due to demand from affluent rulers, becoming highly sought after as objet d'art rather than practical vessels.
A cornerstone of this technique involves slip casting and the addition of thick blue slip during drying. Key characteristics include distinctive geometric patterns, stylized floral motifs, depictions of fruits, birds (particularly qavi, or peacock) inlaid under a shiny white glaze layer, sometimes creating vibrant contrasts – often called 'daylight pottery' for its best appearance.
This intricate process demands immense skill to master the chemical reactions involved in achieving perfect shades and subtle iridescence through patination. Originating centuries ago as a celebratory craft from royal courts demanding specific colours on their wares, blue pottery has evolved into sophisticated handicrafts, valued globally as decorative art or unique souvenirs.
