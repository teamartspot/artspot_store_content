
Chitrakoot is a recognized form of Indian folk art primarily associated with sculpting deities from sacred imagery or 'sacred groves'. However, its scope extends significantly to the intricate production of traditional wooden and pith (often used interchangeably in common parlance) toys. This craft originates mainly from central India's Chhattisgarh state but has spread across regions including Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, Gujarat, Rajasthan, Tripura, and Assam.

While rooted in Rajasthani miniature painting techniques like Patola or Bagar art depicting temple scenes, Chitrakoot evolved into these distinct forms of folk representation. The origin often traces back to the rich devotional traditions fueling deity worship across northern, western, eastern, central, and southern India from around the late 17th century onwards.

These figures transitioned seamlessly into a diverse array of toys for children. Using locally sourced wood or pith (the inner part of fruits like coconut or jackfruit), artisans carve round, cylindrical segments bearing intricate carvings and paintings depicting deities such as Lakshmi Narsimha, Mahavirji, Hanuman, Ganesha, or scenes from popular epics like the Ramayana.

The craft showcases detailed carving skills followed by miniature-style painting with vibrant pigments on an oil base. Beyond religious icons, artisans also create Pith/Block designs known as Nata Koot and Kalagandhakoot featuring local fables and deities, alongside other traditional toys. This forms a vital link connecting historical artistry to contemporary play objects, preserving craftsmanship across generations while catering to children's imagination.
