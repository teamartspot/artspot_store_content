
Andhra Pradesh, a state rich with vibrant folk traditions, also boasts a unique form of theatrical entertainment known as leather puppetry. Originating from simple storytelling needs within the broader context of Andhra's folk arts during harvest festivals like Shigmo (Uttara Vinayaka), this art evolved quite differently from its more famous clay cousin, the Gavari mud puppet.

Unlike intricate shadow plays elsewhere, Andhra leather puppets are primarily used in travelling troupes during daytime performances open to communities. The transition likely occurred because clay figures were difficult to transport and might break during energetic dancing sequences inherent in many Katha dance-drama forms. This led to the development where larger-than-life, simplified human figures could be easily handled on stage.

Master artisans craft these puppets from calfskin or historically, antelope skin (Mahara jala), treating it with oils or ghee for softness and flexibility before stamping designs onto a wooden base plate – 'Kesu Devata'. Intricate detailing is done by hand, sometimes involving multiple layers glued together. They incorporate subtle leather lacings under the costume skirts for expressive movement.

These puppets serve as conduits for traditional narratives focusing on human emotions (love, anger), nature's beauty or cruelty, complex social issues, and temple deities' pastimes ('Lila'). While perhaps less globally renowned than shadow puppetry, Andhra Pradesh’s contribution through these larger, interactive community theatre figures adds a vital thread to India's rich tapestry of traditional performing arts.
