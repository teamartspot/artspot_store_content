
Mysore Rosewood Inlay is one of India's most renowned handicrafts, deeply rooted in the cultural heritage of Mysore, Karnataka. This intricate art form involves embedding contrasting materials into dark rosewood panels to create visually stunning designs.

The pieces are primarily designed as decorative borders or *kote* work for other items like Sharada temples (wooden boxes), fruit stands (*alibaug*, though often made with stone base), frames, and musical instruments such as veena fret boards. Often depicting scenes from Indian mythology, especially Persian-influenced ones like Shahnameh characters, royal portraits, floral motifs *kote* elephants or peacocks are common subjects.

The characteristic dark red-purple wood forms a dramatic backdrop for contrasting colours and intricate patterns. Traditionally, materials included ivory (*kajjali kote*), tortoiseshell (now rarely used due to conservation concerns), mother-of-pearl (meenakari work found in some pieces), brass or bronze wire latticework, and seed beads made from coconut shell meal mixed with gum.

Crafted by skilled artisans using diamond points for meticulous drilling into the rosewood, then filling recesses with chiselled decorative patterns of contrasting materials. This *jadau* craft involves polishing these inlays to a brilliant shine while maintaining the rich grain of the rosewood itself as its primary appeal and signature feature.

The enduring popularity and craftsmanship continue through generations of artisans supported by Mysore's royal patronage legacy ensuring that Rosewood Inlay remains a celebrated symbol Indian artistic excellence.
