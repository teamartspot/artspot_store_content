
Puan sarees trace their origins back to ancient Kancheepuram in Tamil Nadu, initially known as Tantuvals or silk crepe works. Their history began with the evolution of damier texture weaves on pure silk fabrics, distinct from traditional Paithani brocades, around a thousand years ago.

The development continued flourishingly during later dynasties, particularly in Mysore (formerly Kanadian). The Chola and Pandya empires fostered patronage for intricate textile arts. However, it was under the Mysorean kings of the 18th century that Puan silk sarees truly blossomed into a renowned handloom art form.

During this golden age from 1760 onwards, silkworm rearing and complex weaving techniques advanced significantly in areas like Silk City and Srirangapatna. Weavers perfected elaborate designs woven directly on the gold zari, known as pata-pattai or *paatu peetavan kootu*, meaning intricate temple-pattern gold thread work.

While the core creation remains rooted in pure handwoven silk with detailed gold borders, Puan sarees also absorbed influences from European designs for damasks and brocades prevalent during British rule. This shaped their broader aesthetic over time alongside timeless motifs like floral vine creeper patterns (*rangoli*), temple pillars, intricate paisley (*mango variety set work* or *paal kootu*), peacock feathers (*muhandirangadi* design named after the ruler's lineage 'Muddayanagudi'), and mythological figures.

Puan sarees, recognized by their unique texture rather than a single motif, stand as emblems of refined Indian craftsmanship. Evolving from simple silk textures in Kancheepuram to intricate masterpieces in Mysore, they hold significant historical weight and remain treasured globally for their beauty, luxury, and complex artistic execution on pure handwoven silk fabric with detailed gold warp borders (kootu). Their heritage is a blend of classical Indian aesthetics and the refined techniques nurtured within specific royal courts.
