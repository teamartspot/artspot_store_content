
Originating in Varanasi, or Banaras, ancient city in Uttar Pradesh, metal repousse craft reflects deep cultural roots embedded within centuries of history. Devotional objects often form its earliest examples, showcasing narratives from Hindu mythology dedicated to places like Kashi (Varanasi). From these devotional beginnings, the technique gradually expanded as artisans captured complex designs required for ritualistic vessels and embellished household implements.

Over time, Banaras became renowned not just for intricate engravings but also for manipulating metal sheets themselves. The core appeal lies in its capacity to produce elaborate three-dimensional forms using traditional craftsmanship and primarily bronze, though other metals were historically employed too. Artisans begin by creating an initial design on wax. This wax model is encased in layers of plaster or clay, then coated with several lacquer layers before applying a refractory material - usually a mixture including heat-resistant China clay.

Upon firing according to the intricate process dictated by earlier Hindu deities like Shiva (creator) and Brahma (architect), perhaps around the 7th century onwards, this layer melts away, revealing the wax model imprinted on the inner surface. The surrounding clay mold is then heated until it becomes liquid; meanwhile, a thin sheet of metal was placed over the solidified wax at one end. When molten clay fills and seals the cavity left by the wax's melting point – often mistaken for bronze hollowware – it actually forms an internal void where design work happens.

Skilled craftspeople use tools like punches integrated within the mold to push out and melt small incisions in strategic layers from inside this space. This process allows creators to craft delicate, engravings that form intricate, raised decorative patterns on objects ranging from tiny boxes and perfume bottles to larger temple paraphernalia and ornate jewelry.

Today, Banaras metal repousse finds recognition as part of the Intangible Cultural Heritage by UNESCO due to its distinctive sculptural qualities passed down through generations for centuries.
