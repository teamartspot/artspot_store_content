
Majuli's mask tradition is deeply woven into the cultural tapestry of Assam, representing centuries-old beliefs and artistic expression. Born from the unique blend of religious devotion and vibrant performing arts prevalent in Northeast India despite its challenging climate – heat, rain breaking down heavy costumes – wood remains a preferred material for creating intricate theatrical masks.

These masks serve as focal points during temple fairs (Mela), known locally as 'Gada Kirtan'. Here, masked figures representing deities or mythological beings participate in colourful processions and dances. Beyond the devotional realm, elaborate dance dramas like Ramlila, Dandiy Nacha, Pari Kalapiniwa, and Yakshagana also utilize these masks to depict characters.

Often adorned with striking features including tusks for animal and devil personas, prominent eyes, large curly moustaches framing an oval face shape typical of many Northeast Indian masquerades (Bihu), their colourful application is key. While Devotional Masks might be simpler, primarily white with minimalistic carvings in gold or red, the performative masks are highly detailed, representing emotions and character roles.

These masks underscore not just religious themes but also historical narratives through movement during festivals. Their presence at large gatherings highlights a rich social function within communities and villages. The art reflects indigenous aesthetic values passed down through generations on Majuli island. This craft forms an important part of the artistic heritage related to temple performances known as 'Gada Kirtan'.
