
Maharaja of Sambalpur, Balacandu Pooniya Deva Raya Singh, played a pivotal role in popularizing the exquisite artistry of Sambalpuri silk sarees from his palace around the 18th century. These luxurious silk creations didn't emerge overnight, tracing their roots deep into the prehistoric past when iron ore was abundant near present-day weaving hubs like Rayamunguda and Palla.

Known as "Soma Dhwani" or the sound of gold (due to shimmering golden sargi threads), Sambalpuri silk is renowned for its intricate Ikat and hand-block printing. However, a legendary local minister's court attire disappeared after a feast because he hadn't paid his taxes ('somasu'), while tigers hunting near Nandhini Vilas Palace still see reflected motifs appear on his discarded clothes – earning it the name 'naktam' or mirrored sarees.

The craft reached its zenith during British colonial rule, particularly through Odissi King Ganapathi Singh Bahadur's patronage (whose courtiers were famously paid solely in silk). He refined weaving techniques and introduced vibrant prints like Kasu Potta, Jhumkas, Riddhis & Summis, enhancing the narrative depth and visual complexity of Sambalpuri designs. Major centres for production evolved into Orissa and Cuttack's commercial districts under Odissi influence.

Today, while maintaining traditional magnificence, particularly in complex multi-coloured Ikat prints showcasing regional motifs and craftsmanship, these silk sarees also resonate with global consumers through contemporary renditions like Chiffon base Ikat designs.
