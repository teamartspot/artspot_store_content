
Manjushri paintings stand as unique icons within Buddhist art, particularly prominent in Tibetan collections and associated with Nalanda Mahavihara in India, historically a major center for her depiction. Her name translates roughly as "the Man of the Heroic Strength/Skill" or simply "Hero-Victorious."

Often shown standing on a lotus base, sometimes depicted subduing fearsome beings from her lion throne (derived from stories like killing Māra), she symbolizes transcendent wisdom and knowledge that cuts through ignorance (*prajñā*). Her mount, the snow leopard *sngon*, an animal rarely seen south of Tibet, adds a distinct regional character.

These paintings are primarily associated with Tantric Buddhism. During its spread via trade routes (notably the Silk Road from India to Central Asia) and monastic connections around 1000 CE onwards, her image propagated strongly alongside esoteric texts. Legends attribute early Tantric transmission to figures like Padmasambhava acquiring teachings through encounters with Manjushri. In visual terms, she often holds a sword made of light, representing pure knowing cutting ignorance.

The specific iconography linking Manjushri directly to Nalanda reinforces her role as the embodiment of profound *wisdom* cultivated in that great university. These artworks continue, through thangka forms and other media, preserving this symbolism – knowledge perched upon spiritual liberation, presented for contemplation across the ages.
