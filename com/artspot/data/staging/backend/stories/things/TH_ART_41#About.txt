
Considered one of India's most celebrated regional folk art forms, Kangra miniature painting flourishes from the northern state of Himachal Pradesh, notably in districts like Kangra itself and Bilaspur (formerly known as Sirmur). Originating primarily in the courtly settings of Palampur in Sirmur during the 18th century, it swiftly gained royal patronage across the region. What sets Kangra art apart is its unique fusion and reinterpretation of Hindu devotional iconography with elements derived from Tibetan Buddhist thangka traditions.

While some scholars trace its roots further back to medieval times, its definitive form blossomed under Rajput influence after Tipu Sultan's invasion destroyed workshops in Mysore and Madurai. Themes typically revolve around depicting gods, goddesses (especially Radha and Krishna), scenes from epics like the Ramayana or Mahabharata often centering on love narratives, courtly life, flora and fauna, or events linked to local rulers.

Technically masterful, Kangra paintings are executed in opaque tempera onto handmade paper using locally sourced red precipitate for skin tones – a key identifier – and mineral pigments for vibrant colors like indigo. Intricate gold leaf work on intricate backgrounds enhances their beauty significantly; the rich blue skies often seen beneath figures contribute to its dreamy, atmospheric quality.

Known also as 'Sher ka kart' (painting of the tiger king), it earned international acclaim in the 19th century and was declared a National Handicrafts Federation of India Living Treasure item. Its distinctive style continues to captivate audiences worldwide with its ethereal beauty and blend of spiritual devotion and romantic narrative.
