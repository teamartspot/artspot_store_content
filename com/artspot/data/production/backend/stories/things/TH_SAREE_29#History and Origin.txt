
What are known as Bandhej sarees today originated in Hyderabad. It's generally believed they were developed over time by weavers influenced, perhaps initially directly inspired by, the motifs of earlier resist-dyeing techniques used for saris like Paithani or Chikka Georgette from nearby regions.

The defining feature is not just a complex design but the specific method: hand-painted silk onto heavy muslin fabric. Weavers use multiple bichrome yarns where they create patterns that act as resist, preventing the dye from reaching them and thus preserving their original white or contrasting colour against the deep hues laid down in between.

The history is layered; these techniques owe much to the influence of Mughal Persian art motifs via Gujarat's Ajrakh tradition arriving through royal patronage. Over centuries, weavers adapted and refined this approach locally. Initially perhaps simpler variations arose from this assimilation before evolving into the intricate, colourful Bandhej patterns seen today.

Developed significantly since the 1950s – particularly flourishing in Kurnool using its renowned silk quality – and later gaining prominence after Hyderabad became part of Andhra Pradesh, traditional Bandhej sarees evolved distinct characteristics. This unique form of hand-painted weaving represents a rich tradition within south Indian textile art, distinguished by its intricate motifs and distinctive resist-dyeing technique specific to this craft.
