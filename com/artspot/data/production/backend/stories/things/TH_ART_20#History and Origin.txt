
Chhau, a vibrant Indian tribal dance form deeply rooted in both Odisha’s rich traditions and martial arts heritage, owes much of its identity to the iconic Chhau mask. Emerging from communities like those residing near Chausa village during medieval times, though now performed widely across regions including Odisha, West Bengal (Murshia), Jharkhand, and Bihar, these masks are integral costumes.

Their origins lie far back in history, long predating their theatrical use. Primarily employed in ancient tribal rituals like Yakshaagya ceremonies concerning rain gods and spirits for community well-being, Nagabandha serpent dances, animal appeasement rites (Shakthisvapu), and exorcism performances against evil forces, the masks represented deities or characters crucial to these beliefs.

As Chhau dance evolved into a performance art form around temple festivals such as Rath Yatra, Kirtan Yata, and Dol Yata celebrations in Udayagiri hills near Bhubaneswar starting from the 13th century (though perhaps fully developed later), artists continued borrowing this mask tradition. They used it not just for ritual purposes but also to portray narrative characters directly within temple episodes or stories like 'Chhatrasal's escape' during energetic performances.

Today, these masks remain central visual elements in Chhau. Made traditionally from mud and wood using rudimentary tools, or now commonly cardboard covered with cloth or mica sheets (for lighter effects), they feature striking geometric patterns, bold eye and mouth holes, sometimes adorned with jewels like nose rings or forehead bindis. They dramatically blend folk culture and ancient traditions, preserving the essence of tribal history and religious epics from centuries past through performance art.
