
Kashmiri walnut wood carving is an ancient craft traditionally associated with the Kashmir Valley. Its origins trace back likely to Persian artisans fleeing invasions centuries ago, who brought their skills in search of refuge or work. These craftsmen discovered walnut wood's grain pattern closely resembled esteemed rosewood inlays used in Persian furniture and musical instruments; rosewood was scarce elsewhere. They began chiseling intricate patterns onto the robust Kashmiri walnut.

The craft flourished under Mughal patronage, particularly during the reigns of Emperor Jahangir (16th century) and Shah Jahan (late 17th-early 18th centuries). Demand for exquisite furniture inlaid with mother-of-pearl, ivory, jasper, and precious woods encouraged artisans to perfect the technique. Complex geometric designs, floral medleys, and scenes inspired by Persian art dominate this period, executed on a variety of walnut pieces.

Continuing under Sikh rule and later integrating into princely India during British administration preserved its distinctiveness even when other regional arts shifted towards fretwork or ivory carving for royalty. The craft often served functional objects like cabs (wooden boxes with hinged lids) and musical instruments such as santurs, alongside intricate decorative panels.

Today, artisans from the Kashmiri Pandit community are noted exponents of this fine art form. Though historical demand patterns have shifted away from royal use due to cost constraints in a post-colonial economy focusing on mass production, the rich history remains etched onto every finely carved nutwood creation crafted locally.
