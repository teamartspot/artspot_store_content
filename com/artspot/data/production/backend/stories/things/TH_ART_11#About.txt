
Bikaner painting originates from the arid landscapes of Rajasthan, India, specifically associated with Jodhpur and its surrounding territories like Bikaner and parts of Marwar, often considered a precursor or related style within the broader Rajasthani school. Its characteristic brilliance defines this art form.

The defining feature is its extraordinary palette – hues derived not only from mineral pigments but also unique natural sources like beetroot for intense reds and leaves for emerald greens. This results in exceptionally vibrant colours, especially noticeable during festive periods when these themes find prominence on doors or windows, creating striking visual impacts against the colourful backgrounds.

Thematically, Bikaner paintings are diverse. While historical religious scenes from Puranas and Hindu epics featuring deities exist, its hallmark today is courtly portraiture – capturing the profiles of royalty, aristocracy, including men in elaborate attire and women, famously 'Ghugniyan' or royal ladies adorned with jewels like the 'Kalika', a unique handbag. Scenes from everyday life are also common.

Technically, artists known as 'Bichitra Patuas' create these works on plain fabrics (cotton canvas often stencilled for doors) and sometimes scrolls ('paandav ka phool'), using tempera or lacquer paint applied via fine brushes through a resist technique involving wax. Although no longer exclusively created by temples, this traditional method continues.

Bikaner painting, particularly its door art known as 'mundan', offers rich narratives – documenting family histories, honouring ancestors, and providing vibrant decoration. Its legacy showcases the ingenuity of Rajasthani artists in adapting ancient techniques to create works both decorative and enduringly captivating.
