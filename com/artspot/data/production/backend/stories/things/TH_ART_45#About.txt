
Kashmir Paper Mache is a unique folk art hailing from the culturally rich region of Jammu and Kashmir, India. While sharing fundamental techniques with other paper mache traditions globally, it possesses a distinct local character rooted in regional aesthetics and spirituality.

The craft involves creating durable objects using a base layer typically coated with plaster or mud – often cow dung mixed with ash – upon which multiple layers of dampened, glued-down recycled paper are applied. This forms the structural skeleton of intricate designs that range from decorative masks to festival structures. Once several layers are dry, colourful dyes derived traditionally from natural materials like plants and minerals come alive across these surfaces.

The motifs found in Kashmir Paper Mache often reflect religious beliefs or seasonal celebrations. Masks frequently depict deities or mythical creatures central to local legends, while other objects might portray various animals known by their medicinal properties: the goat for guggul oil, an eagle for its association with freedom and the sun god 'Pushan'. These designs are crafted with meticulous detail.

This art form is more than just creation; it's a vibrant part of Kashmiri culture. The craft itself relies on local expertise and resources to produce, maintaining a strong connection between artisans and their traditional environment. It embodies the distinctive artistic temperament, colours, and craftsmanship associated specifically with this northern Indian region, often showcased during Navratri festivities alongside other arts like Channar Poothu.
