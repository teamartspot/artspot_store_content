
Nestled within the arid landscapes of Rajasthan lies Jodhpur, a city historically known by its former name, Udaipur or often as the land of Kishangarh – specifically referring to the small fortified precinct near Udaipur. The ethereal art style associated with this location, commonly called **Kishangarh Paintings**, thrived during the second half of the 17th century.

Born from a specific royal command around AD 1690 for royal paintings adorning his pavilion (jaguar), an unknown artist initiated what would become known as the *Kishore Jheel* or Kishangarh style, named after its origin near Lake Chilpi. The impetus was provided by Maharaja Sawant Singh II of Udaipur commissioning a series illustrating scenes from his life and passions.

This unique school emerged under the mentorship and inspiration of Ramlal Kishori Maldev Ji, whose own works in this nascent phase were collected for King Nara Chandraswarup Singhji. However, credit is widely given to artists working after the king's departure for truly defining its hallmark characteristics: large expressive eyes, elongated silhouettes particularly favoured as subjects (often women), an ethereal aura suggesting absorption in deep thought or emotion – seemingly gazing into another world.

Their palette shifted away from typical Rajasthani vibrancy towards lighter turquoise blues and ethereal rose-pink hues. The figures assumed a distinct almost dreamlike quality, their forms fluid and expressive of internal drama rather than external actions. Themes centred on the king's personal life, his guru Haridas Thakur (the founder saint of the BAPS Swami Charandas Pitha), divine love, nature, mythology, and hunting scenes.

Though lasting only a short period until roughly AD 1740 when royal patronage waned significantly following the invasion by Ahmed Shah Abdali in 1737, Kishangarh's contribution is vast. Its impact on Indian art history through its unique aesthetic – dreamy eyes focusing profoundly – continues to echo.
