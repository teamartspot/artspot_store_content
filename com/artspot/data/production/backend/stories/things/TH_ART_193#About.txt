
Deep within the verdant landscapes of Uttarakhand, lies an art form etched into history itself – Uttarakhand Likhai. More than just carving wood, this technique embodies a story narrating ancient Himalayan life and spirituality. Essentially meaning "wood relieves" or surface carvings (contrasted with Chikani ka jada which uses chisels directly on stone or metal), it requires intricate hollowing out from materials like sal wood to create multi-panel, often rectangular compositions telling tales through negative space.

Likhai masterfully captures scenes of profound local significance. Its motifs predominantly feature mythological deities such as Shiva and Parvati, the enchanting Ganga goddesses, celestial beings tied in love stories or dances (like Bilwadevi), and daily life depictions – a woodcutter’s journey to the forest’s edge, perhaps children playing in an old mango grove. These figures often stand with characteristic poses against symbolic backgrounds like lotus ponds or mountain slopes.

Artisans known as Likha Sthambhikars (sculptors of reliefs) primarily carve in salwood. Famous carvers such as Jogi Shankar from Almora, a master craftsman and teacher at the State Handicrafts Emporium, have popularized distinct regional styles, notably through intricate depictions like Bilvadevi – with detailed swirling hair patterns signifying her divine ecstasy.

The preservation efforts of institutions are slowly sustaining this delicate craft. But its essence remains very much alive in the hands of village artisans across towns like Kausambi and Ramnabhatar. For lovers of art seeking narratives steeped not just in stone but organic contours narrating Hindu pantheons' divine tales, Uttarakhand Likhai offers profound visual storytelling on wood surfaces.
