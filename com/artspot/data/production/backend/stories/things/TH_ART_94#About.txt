
Originating from the vibrant town of Thanjavur (Thiruvanaikoovil) in Tamil Nadu's Tirunelveli district, these classic dolls are a testament to centuries-old craftsmanship deeply woven into South India's rich artistic heritage. Though their origins might trace back slightly north towards ancient Chola kingdom traditions associated with 'Kolaveri' art and temple rituals, the contemporary Thanjavur doll is most strongly linked to this Tanjore city.

For generations, artisans in Thanjavur meticulously crafted dolls as part of village-level art practices alongside religious icons. The transition began post-Rajasthan's integration into broader tourism promotion systems likely after India gained independence; artists needed simpler commercial sculptures accessible to mass markets. The doll emerged as an answer – a small figure capable of being colored and finished rapidly without sacrificing traditional aesthetics.

The materials primarily involve locally sourced wood, typically jackfruit tree log cores treated for durability followed by layered cloth dolls sometimes called 'manushyai'. Hand-sculpting using carving tools forms the base layer. Then meticulous hand-painting with naphthol colours (a synthetic range) follows to achieve fine brushwork and distinctive patterns against colourful backgrounds.

Today, they are internationally recognized symbols of Indian folk art – simple creations yet captivating in detail and expression. Each doll tells a story implicitly through its attire or accessories reflecting regional attire like sari or dhoti variations along with ornaments often including flower garlands (julipparthu), anklets, bangles, headgear representing specific costumes etc., even depicting regional customs. Acquired by collectors and tourists alike they grace displays globally celebrating Indian craftsmanship diversity
