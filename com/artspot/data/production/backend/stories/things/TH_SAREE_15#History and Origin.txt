
Kanjeevaram silk sarees, also known colloquially as 'Salem Silks' or associated with the Chettiar community's legacy, have a rich and storied history despite their name evoking connections to Karaikkudi in Tamil Nadu. Their true epicentre of origin is Varanasi (Benares), West Bengal.

Developed during British colonial rule around the 18th-19th centuries, these sarees emerged as high-quality silk silks from Mysore worms cultivated near their traditional koshies or silkworm clutches). The intricate 'brocade' weft work of Benarusiyars found fertile ground here. Initially woven in places like Poondurai (Pondicherry) and Salem, they quickly gained prominence.

Known for exceptional thickness ('mulmul'), luster, and durability due to high silk count, Kanjeevaram silk brocades feature complex zari decoration using pure gold thread, sometimes coloured silk threads too. Motifs often draw from temple carvings (mandapams), flowers (gandantavasali petals, nelli flower), devotional scenes ('tutuppaayi'), mythological figures, and traditional borders.

Kanjeevaram is renowned for its heavy body despite the fine weave, achieved using high thread count. The 'patola' method involves stopping zari work each week to add golden thread length from pure gold yarns twisted with copper or zinc-coated ones ('gold coated'), ensuring rich colour absorption and longevity against fading and tarnishing.

These sarees were originally reserved for temple use and upper-middle-class families but became iconic symbols of South Indian opulence and heritage over time.
