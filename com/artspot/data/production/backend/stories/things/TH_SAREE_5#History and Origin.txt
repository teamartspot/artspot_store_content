
Often called 'the Chamba Rumal of Gujarat', Ajrakh has a rich legacy intertwined with hand-painted block printing. Originating primarily from the coastal regions of present-day Saurashtra (Bharuch district), its name combines 'Ajeera' (a type of coarse cloth) and 'Akha' (possibly referencing Akbar's era or the distinct colours). The exact geographical starting point remains debated, but its earliest forms date back centuries.

The distinctive geometric blue-on-green motifs likely evolved locally from traditional Saurashtra weaving patterns. Text records mention indigo printing techniques similar to Ajrakh in texts as early as 1604 AD (possibly linked to Gujarat), while evidence shows the craft becoming prominent much earlier, perhaps even predating that date range.

Historically, these intricate geometric designs began appearing on coarse cotton saris around medieval times. While Mughals are sometimes wrongly credited with inventing all block print motifs, Ajrakh spread wider via trade routes from Gujarat and Rajasthan during the 17th century onwards. Patola sarees from Surat/Daman Ganga used silk reeds for resist-dyeing on zari fabrics, demanding greater precision than typical fabric saris, but much older! Simpler blue-on-green cotton saris with Ajrakh patterns gained popularity later.

These specific motifs first became prominent in Gujarat's textile trade perhaps around the 13th-14th century but likely evolved significantly over centuries. The unique dyed and printed coir bags suggest an ancient, experimental phase before focusing on fabrics for saris or men's lungi trousers (Kurta).
