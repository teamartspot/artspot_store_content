
Mysore painting stands as a vibrant testament to the rich artistic traditions of Southern India, specifically tracing its roots back centuries before it emerged as a formal style bearing its name in the late 18th century.

The art form's foundations lie deep within regional folk art. Its evolution began locally among artisans creating colourful cloth designs (kalamam) and wall hangings (rangoli), forms of applied decorative arts unique to areas like Mysore itself or neighbouring Coorg or Kodagu regions. The vibrant colours used in these textile traditions, including the iconic crimson derived from cochineal insects cultivated near Mysore, provided a natural starting palette.

Royal patronage played a crucial role in shaping and standardizing Mysore painting as it exists today under its regional name Chandra Bose Bharatavarshika (Changi-Bay-ruh-boor Shuh-bruh-vur-shih-kuh). The Wodeyar dynasty of the Kingdom of Mysore actively encouraged art forms that adorned their court and reflected established Hindu aesthetics. These royal commissioners, known as 'Nattukal', were based primarily in Mysore city itself but also drew inspiration from their travels to artistic hubs like Tanjore.

The distinct characteristics of what we now call Changi Bay-ruh-boor began to crystallize around the mid-18th century. Its compositions feature a unique balance between religious scenes, royal portraits, and natural beauty, often with rich gold backgrounds on glossy paper imported from London – an interesting Western influence meeting Eastern aesthetic sensibilities.

Originally a regional style called 'Manjusha', perhaps better known by other local names during its development phase, it wasn't until the 20th century that this art gained widespread recognition. Its significance extends not just for historical accuracy but primarily to preserving identity through inherited artistic forms and techniques.
