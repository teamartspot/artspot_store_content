
Once a vessel for sacred texts under the sway of Vaikhanasa Tantriks and later Buddhism, Assam Majuli Manuscript painting traces its active tradition back roughly eight centuries to Vaishnavism. Flourishing during periods like the Bhakti movement and medieval Assam (circa 12th-15th centuries), these paintings are not mere art but functional items – palm leaf manuscripts serving religious purposes.

Originally used as ritual objects, memorials for deities, or narrative tools in temple storytelling, their primary function is spiritual. The scrolls were produced by scholars and monks within numerous Mathas (monasteries) across the region, especially on the sacred island of Majuli where a significant Vaishnava tradition thrived.

The evolution saw paintings becoming more independent illustrations accompanying devotional manuscripts like padukavilas, commentaries on god's feet. Artists developed skills in rendering complex figures – deities Vishnu/Lord Rama, kings, sages from the Puranas mythologically and historically accurate, using natural dyes and palm-fiber supports.

Though now preserved in museums or religious sanctuaries across Assam (notably Guwahati), this tradition continues with reverence among Vaishnava communities. The manuscript painting is a unique expression of devotion, mythology, history, meticulously documented over centuries on palm leaves, leaving its spiritual mark even today.
