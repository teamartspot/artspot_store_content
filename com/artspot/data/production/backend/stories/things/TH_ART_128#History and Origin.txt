
# Saharanpur Wood Craft

The origin of Saharanpur wood craft traces back to the small town of Balangir in eastern Uttar Pradesh, India, rather than Saharanpur itself (though the name might have become prominent elsewhere). Its earliest known association is with **Baba Jaswant Singh**, a fifteenth-century musician and devotee belonging to the Bhakti movement (specifically Vaishnavism) who hailed from this region. He was renowned for crafting musical instruments, particularly veena-like lutes called 'Sarang' or 'Chakri', directly on trees – often using mulberry – eliminating the need for separate body and neck pieces nailed together.

This unique method involved hollowing out one main log for a fretless fingerboard and attaching the curved arm (guru) with specially carved nuts. The guru typically features intricate depictions of deities, animals ('ghoomar' motifs), sacred flora like karanja or peepal trees, and complex scriptural writings alongside musical notation symbols inscribed directly onto its surface.

While credited largely to Baba Jaswant Singh for innovation around 1450 CE, the craft represents a continuation of older Indian traditions of lute making ('vina'). Saharanpur wood craft isn't just about instruments; it's intrinsically linked to Sufi devotional music as well. The direct carving onto the tree is both time-consuming and requires significant skill mastery due to its reliance on natural curvature.

The distinctive feature lies in carving musical notation directly onto each nut, forming intricate patterns that denote specific melodic movements or ragas. Each 'guru' carves out a unique sound quality when paired with various fretless fingerboards according to established families of tunes associated for centuries.
