
Tiruchanur's paper mache craft reflects not just artistic ingenuity but also a response to local material constraints and, importantly, the era following British colonial rule. This period saw significant economic changes for coastal artisans who previously supplied European markets.

The specific geographical context matters; Tiruchanur is located in present-day Tiruchirappalli district of Tamil Nadu. Its vibrant Chettiar community culture provided fertile ground for such innovations. Initially termed 'Kalichedukal' or 'coconut husk art', the craft demonstrates adaptation rather than purely ancient tradition.

Artisans adopted a paper-based medium, perhaps as an economical alternative after trade opportunities shifted locally, transforming discarded cardboard into visually compelling artwork featuring stylised figures and vibrant colours, executed uniquely there.

While techniques related to traditional south Indian murals existed, the modern form of Tiruchanur paper mache likely emerged later. Its creator V. Sureshbabu Nair consciously sought not just a novel medium but also aesthetic recognition for his innovation within India, distinct from export-based work. Today, it serves as a vital link to Tanjore's evolving artistic landscape and showcases how indigenous creativity can rise anew even in changing economic times.
