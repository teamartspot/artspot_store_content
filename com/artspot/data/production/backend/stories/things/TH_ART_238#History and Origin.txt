
The *Nicobari Canoe*, locally known as **Hodi**, stands as a testament to indigenous ingenuity in the Andaman & Nicobar Islands.

Crafted traditionally from large mangrove tree trunks, often specifically the Intex or Baloo tree (*Nagea oldhamii*), this single-seated dugout canoe is not merely transport; it's intrinsically linked to the identity and livelihood of communities like the Neso tribe in the Nicobars.

Its history mirrors that of outrigger craft throughout Southeast Asia, likely evolving for local conditions over centuries. Archaeological findings on both sides, including stone anchors dating back potentially 3500 years near Dwarka in Gujarat, provide evidence for canoe-based seafaring. The Hodi thrived long before modern boats arrived with colonial ships and later outboard motors eroding its traditional role.

Culturally, the single-man capacity held great significance among certain Nicobari clans (jaths). While initially used primarily for fishing close to shore, it served crucially as a ceremonial transport vessel or raptorial boat in specific rituals. It represented status and was essential for transporting important figures. The journey of building and launching a Hodi itself became an event, with meticulous craftsmanship required.

Today, while less vital for the demanding work voyages between central Nicobar, Andaman, and mainland India that require larger craft like outboats or trishtapu (evening boat), the tradition persists in some areas. The Hodi remains a vital cultural artifact, symbolizing past traditions of independent coastal travel and embodying ancestral skills preserved for posterity against the currents of modern change.
