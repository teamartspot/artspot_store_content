
Sikki art refers to intricate artworks created by folding grass stems into elaborate patterns. These folded pieces are then intricately interwoven together using fine cords or grass threads.

The origin and history trace back mainly to West African countries, particularly Ghana (known locally as _Adinkra_). While the exact beginnings are difficult to pinpoint definitively due to anonymity within tribal traditions across various regions like northern Ghana, it's believed to have been developed over several generations. The art form likely arose alongside sophisticated basket weaving techniques and a rich tradition of storytelling through symbols.

Historically, while its specific origins may be old, Sikki gained broader recognition in the modern era during or after the colonial period, although gaining wider international awareness much later perhaps as part of African contemporary craft movement. There isn't necessarily one definitive story; it likely evolved independently across different communities over centuries as an artistic expression.

Traditional uses often involved decorative elements adorning huts or even forming temporary walls during significant events like funerals and festivals (serving a role comparable to Adinkra symbols). It represented complex ideas, patterns of existence, and sometimes served ceremonial purposes requiring specific care due to its delicate nature. While older examples exist within their communities, much contemporary work uses _Saccharum burrellii_ or 'wild grass' near Accra, which differs slightly from the historical original materials reported by those who practice it traditionally and might offer better durability than other local stems.
