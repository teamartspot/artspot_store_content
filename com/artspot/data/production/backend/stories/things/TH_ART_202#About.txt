
The Majuli mask, a vibrant symbol of Assam's rich cultural heritage, especially from the floating island of Majuli in the Brahmaputra River, is an essential part of various traditional dances and ceremonies. Crafted predominantly in villages like Satara alongside Khudong, these masks are traditionally made by applying layers of white paste prepared from bamboo sap onto large, circular wooden disks known as 'patachitra'.

These hand-painted, multi-coloured masks serve both aesthetic and functional purposes. While they can depict diverse mythological characters from Hindu epics like Ramayana or Mahabharat, typically they portray deities associated with the Vaishnavite sect prevalent in Assam, such as Radha-Krishna, Gopal-Devi, Hanuman, Lakshmi Narasimha, Bhaktivinoda Thakur, and Jagannath Das. A common feature is a prominent lotus blossom ('padma keerthanang'), and the masks usually have large fan-shaped wings protruding from their back, offering dramatic and sometimes intimidating visuals.

Worn during performances of traditional dances like 'Pungkoli' or 'Bhramram', as well as in temple festivals ('visarjan'), the mask provides performers with anonymity. This allows them to portray different characters and maintain focus within the narrative while protecting their identity from being overly exposed during ritual events. Primarily associated with Vaishnavism, these masks are also sometimes used for depicting divine figures linked to other religious traditions like Jainism in Assam. Their status as an intangible cultural heritage of Mantrasangye Sabha has led to UNESCO recognition.

The Majuli mask's rich colours and distinctive winged motifs not only captivate the audience visually but are deeply significant, embodying the complex and sacred narratives that form part of the unique identity passed down through generations in coastal Assam.
