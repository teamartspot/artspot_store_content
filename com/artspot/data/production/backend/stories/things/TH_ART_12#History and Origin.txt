
The art of block printing involves carving text or images onto an often-wooden block, inking the surface, and then pressing it onto material – traditionally fabric – to create a printed design.

Its origins are most commonly attributed to ancient China. Evidence suggests its use might date back as early as the Han Dynasty (around 206 BC). While woodblock printing likely developed initially for text, pictorial printing also emerged around this period or slightly earlier in some Tang dynasty examples (~AD 618 onward), with scholars like Timothy Takeo Fujiwara suggesting artistic possibilities existed before. Here, artists would carve the block close to but not removing all of a particular character's wood where lines should be; ink applied (or sometimes just scraped dry) reveals the raised areas or uses reverse image carving.

These techniques soon gained popularity worldwide as textile art forms. Trade routes disseminated this knowledge, leading artisans in India and medieval Europe, amongst others, to perfect it with different materials, including cloth-covered stencils later known as clichés, or intricate stone lithographic plates. Block printing offered a relatively low-tech but effective way to apply consistent patterns across textiles long before more advanced printing methods were invented.

Its history remains intrinsically linked to textile decoration and the diffusion of cultural aesthetics through these tangible blocks and their resulting imprints.
