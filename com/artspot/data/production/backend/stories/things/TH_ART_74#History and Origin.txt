
Navalgund Durries trace their name back to the word 'Nau', an Arabic loan from Sanskrit for ship/nave, combined with 'Gundi' (a regional variant of 'Durree' meaning rug). While less celebrated than some classic Indian textile designs like Warli Hang or Banaras brocade, Navalgund rugs reflect a distinct history likely tied to coastal regions.

They emerged following the 16th-century Portuguese arrival in India and subsequent Maratha naval expansion dominance (mid-17th C onwards), periods where strong regional marine power shapes were established. These hand-woven floor coverings feature specific geometric patterns deeply connected to this nautical theme, often resembling anchors or stylized ships alongside intricate rope designs using motifs like squares ('Mitti'), dots ('Bindu / Kumkuma'), and wavy lines.

Predominantly found in coastal Maharashtra and parts of Gujarat (especially Surat), Navalgund Durries served not just decorative purposes but also held regional significance. The patterns frequently represent the 'Bhangkar' or *Gujjubhai*, a ship's hull section, symbolizing maritime traditions and trade importance local to these areas.

Originally used by trading communities like the Gujaratis who navigated coastal waters, these durries showcase weaving techniques learned from Portuguese and European influences but developed uniquely into an indigenous product. They remain a testament to India's rich artisan tradition dedicated to durable flooring solutions with potent symbolic connections drawn from its vibrant seafaring past.
