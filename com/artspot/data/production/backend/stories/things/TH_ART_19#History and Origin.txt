
The Cheriyal Scroll, also known as Kalari Koothu, represents a vibrant folk theatre tradition with deep roots in the Madurai region of Tamil Nadu. Its lineage traces back centuries, likely originating from rural performances expressing community beliefs or celebrating agricultural cycles through narratives on cloth scrolls.

What distinguishes this art form is its unique use of materials and themes. The name 'Cheriyal' translates to "sacred grove" or "palmyra forest," leading many scholars to believe the pioneers were Lingayats, Pasis, or Irulars – communities historically linked to forests. However, it gained prominence at royal courts around Madurai's Chandrajeeshwaram temple.

The tradition holds a distinct oral element. Each performance is typically unique; different groups interpret the same story differently each time for new audiences. No single source scrolls exist to copy or reference. The performances follow a standard three-day festival schedule, complete with elaborate rituals involving specific music forms and attire before anyone sees the scroll.

Originally hand-unrolled on palm fronds, these narrative scrolls evolved into larger cloth banners over time, often depicting deities or royal figures but sometimes everyday hero stories (slocalars). Typically around 20-50 feet long, they required two people to display.

The anonymity of practitioners is a cornerstone. Performers don masks and cloaks, allowing audiences to focus solely on the narrative and character dialogue delivered with rhythmic intensity by male actors mimicking emotions through subtle facial movements. Despite regional variations in style and content, this captivating scroll-based dance drama remains alive as a traditional art form today.
