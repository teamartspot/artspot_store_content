
Banaras metal casting, primarily executed in the revered city of Varanasi using bronze or a copper-tin alloy, represents a unique and historically significant craft tradition associated with Hindu mythology. Though distinct from the famed brass crafting on utensils often linked directly to Banaras, this specialized form thrives here.

Characterized by intricate design complexity – far exceeding standard pottery motifs – these crafts are deeply rooted in religious iconography and temple artistry. Items range from ceremonial masks for deities (often carved into master models) to highly decorative stand-alone bronzes (e.g., palaces diya holders), ritualistic vessels, and sometimes household articles. They frequently employ silver ('Chitralay') accents alongside bronze casting.

The traditional method employed is nearly exclusive: the lost-wax technique (*Kshara Kumbhakam*). Artists sculpt a pattern in beeswax on prepared metal frames (plasters), coat it with pottery clay, let sections dry and cure for strength, then bake them. Molten material pours into the hollow wax mold created by breaking up the burned-out original mold, yielding replicas that match *all dimensions*, including highly detailed features and even underglaze painting when applied immediately post-casting.

This craft holds immense cultural significance as it visually connects communities to ancient religious narratives (*itihasa*). It offers crucial employment opportunities for local artisans across generations, preserving traditional skills dependent on complex techniques like plaster modeling with intricate recessed borders, wax application, careful mold construction, and specialist finishing.
