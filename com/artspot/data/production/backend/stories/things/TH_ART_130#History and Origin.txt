
The genesis of Thewa art is deeply intertwined with Manipur's rich mythology and its connection to Buddhism, stemming from a fascinating legend concerning its founding father, Nara Singh Thool. According to lore recounted in the text Panna Dharmavilasini, during a visit to Rajoimbi Temple outside Imphal while serving as a court painter for the Ahom king, he witnessed figures with gold and jewel-like enhancements on their attire from an illuminated mandapa frame.

Mesmerized by this technique of *Nasikya Bhandaraya*, or 'throat filling', Nara Singh Thool requested to know its secrets. Deceived that they were merely motifs on the wooden structure, artisans secretly revealed how they employed gold and paste gold dust meticulously worked upon the brass framework using fine-pointed lap pens made from animal hooves.

Upon his return to Manipur, Nara Singh Thool defied the original *Nasikya* method by creating purely enamel-covered figures – a pivotal moment that birthed traditional Thewa (or 'Thewi'). These initially depicted Buddhist deities in mandapas (wooden pavilions) or temple settings. The core materials are brass or copper, intricately hollowed and shaped like masks before multiple layers of vibrant vitrified enamels are painstakingly applied using fine brushes.

Distinctive motifs include elaborate headpieces often framing eyes resembling the Gotri style, earrings, necklaces with bell-shaped pendants called 'dabring', bracelets (bindi), nose ornaments ('deuh'), large bangles on ankles ('dungaba'), and flowing attire seams. Primarily inspired by Buddhist narratives from texts like Panna Dharmavilasini or other Jathis, Thewa later flourished during the Meitei kingdom and is recognized today as a National Cultural capital art form by the Government of India.
