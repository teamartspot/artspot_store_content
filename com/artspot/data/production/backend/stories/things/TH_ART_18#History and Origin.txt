
Chamba rumal embroidery has its roots in Kashmir, primarily during the late 17th century or early 18th century. Believed to have been developed under royal patronage, perhaps for Nur Jehan herself, these intricate *rumals* (*rūmul*, meaning dishrag) were initially woven rather than embroidered.

The distinctive texture of Chamba rumal is achieved using a subtle weave technique often mistakenly referred to as 'embroidery' in the West. It's not true embroidery thread but involves fine silk weft threads passing through two sets of warp threads woven side by side with different colours, creating raised areas and hollows within the fabric weave itself.

Motifs are incredibly diverse, ranging from traditional Kashmiri mountain patterns and paisley to geometric forms, leaves, flowers (both European and Indian), birds, including mythical ones like charwams. There's often a strong narrative quality through these elements, frequently incorporating designs spelled out using the Persian script 'Ain al-'Arab'.

Known for their complex colour schemes, rich blues contrasting with purples, crimson sashes against dark backgrounds, Chamba rumals blend floral elegance and traditional tales in unique geometric harmony. Historically used as head or arm coverings by Kashmiri women before becoming recognised forms of portable artwork, they served both functional purposes related to social etiquette (covering hair etc.) during times when courtly interaction was customary attire outside the palace gates too.
