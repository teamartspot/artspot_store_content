
Sarthebari metal craft, primarily executed with bronze and wrought iron, traces its origins to the indigenous communities of West Bengal's northern regions, particularly around places like Nāgarhara (near Asansol). Its roots are believed to lie in pre-Hindu/Sanskrit times, later influenced by royal workshops established during the medieval period. However, its distinct identity solidified as a village craft under British colonial administration.

During the mid-19th century, when industries shifted towards urban centers and traditional village crafts faced decline elsewhere due to wage competition from hired labour or factories, Sarthebari artisans in these specific villages continued their traditions independently. They focused mainly on crafting functional agricultural implements – keys, trowels, knives (panchakelis) used for betel leaf chewing, sickles, scythes (kalarjandas), ploughshares, hoes, and buckets.

The craft's unique character lies in its integration into village life during this era of industrial change. The raw materials were typically scrap metal sourced locally or from nearby ruined kilns. This reliance on simple tools, ingenuity, collective effort within the smithy community (which included blacksmiths, forgers, and copper-smiths), and catering to daily needs helped Sarthebari not only survive but thrive in its unique environment, preserving a living link to pre-Victorian times where village industries formed the backbone of society across these regions.
