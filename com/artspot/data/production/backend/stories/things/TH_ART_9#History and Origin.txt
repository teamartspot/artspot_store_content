
Bengal Pattachitra, a vibrant openwork scroll painting tradition flourishing along India's Eastern Coast in West Bengal, traces its origins surprisingly back across the Kritibadhaka hills from Odisha (formerly Orissa). Emerging around the 7th century during the Pallava and Chola dynasties under Dravidian influence was the earlier 'Kolattachitraya' style. These intricate narrative scrolls often depicted devotional Hindu themes, particularly the Bhakti movement's popularisation after Tantrik Buddhism declined.

This art form later found its ideal home in Murshidabad during the 17th century when Ahiravartru royal apprentices learned and refined the craft under Nawab Mir Jafar's court garrison. Their distinct style focused on storytelling, history, mythology, science, geometry – featuring cosmic trees, animals with stylised eyes ('Netrakanta'), alongside Hindu and Buddhist deities, often reflecting Vaishnavism influences.

Master painters like Nandalal Baji passed down techniques orally until mid-20th century. The 1937 Benodebehari Lal Competition at Kala Bhavan formally initiated the modern phase, establishing Pattachitra's unique aesthetics today – rich yellows and reds on royal blue silk or canvas ground, characterised by its beautiful openwork (Jhal drawing). Today, Bengal Pattachitra masters work globally under government patronage, securing its position in India’s intangible cultural heritage.
