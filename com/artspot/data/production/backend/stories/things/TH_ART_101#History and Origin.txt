
# History and Origin: Sandur Lambani

Sandur Lambani is a form of Phello work from Chhattisgarh in central India, specifically evolving within the Sandur tribal region under the influence of specific artists inspired by royal patronage. Its distinctive history involves a blend of age-old techniques with new influences.

The craft's development accelerated during the late 18th century when the British consolidated their control over the area surrounding Ranchi through agencies near the Bail Gandha hills, strategically important for forest produce and the transport route to northern regions like Sandur. This period saw interaction and patronage from royalty that had previously influenced tribal art in neighbouring areas through Gujarat.

Early influences included appliqué work known as Jadau (using gold or silver 'gutta' threads), imported via trade routes. These Mughal-inspired patterns were adapted, giving rise to motifs like 'Kumkuman'. While initially adopted by nobles and kings for their attire during feasts like Navratri, the craft gradually spread through Chhattisgarh villages.

The defining origin story points to an artist named Lambani from Sandur village who refined these techniques. Other nearby tribes such as Dhawal (from Ranchi), Muria, and Halba adopted his style, contributing unique designs while incorporating core elements like elaborate mirror work using gold zari threads ('Gandha'). Jadau appliqué remains central.

This heritage craft continues to thrive, adding prestige texture to traditional Chippa dhuti or Lungis. Today it's also finding acclaim as part of the village textile industry across Chhattisgarh and beyond, responding globally requested designs while preserving its rich roots in indigenous motifs and royal history.
