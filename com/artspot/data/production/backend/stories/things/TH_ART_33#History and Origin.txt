
Ghazipur wall hangings have a history rooted in ancient Indian art traditions, specifically emerging during the early medieval period as sculptured panels designed to be portable. Unlike traditional temple carvings or architectural sculpture, they were intended for public display rather than being part of permanent structures.

Their origin can be traced back around the 5th century CE through influences stemming from Jain and Buddhist dharmashalas (monasteries) in the Ganga valley region, known for intricate stone carving reliefs. These initial forms might have been precursors to moving more freely toward sculptured panels independent of temple carvings.

During successive periods under rulers like Harshvardhana into Hindu dynasties such as those who built temples in regions with sandstone frets, the Ghazipur style developed further from scriptural panels.

By roughly a thousand years ago, during medieval times and likely influenced by Muslim artisans or aesthetic principles introduced then, stone work carving wall hanging sculptures evolved in Mughal palaces into popular luxury items. They were used as decorative elements for interiors and processions of royal families.

The panels remained primarily portable decorative objects rather than inscriptions. This practical form transitioned to a religious style during the medieval period itself or around the start of the Islamic era, likely through interaction with diverse cultures in what is modern-day Jaipur region. Various artisans contributed evolving aesthetics over time until recent heritage preservation efforts revitalized carving wall hanger sculptures as valued craft items today.
