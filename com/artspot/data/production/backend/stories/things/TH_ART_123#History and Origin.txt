
Farrukhabad prints, evoking an ancient heritage beneath their colonial-era name, trace their roots to the city known as **Kashi** during earlier periods. This region in Uttar Pradesh saw a flourishing of textile artistry centuries ago, characterized by intricate hand-block printing techniques on fine fabrics.

Artisans developed distinct motifs deeply intertwined with local religious and cultural narratives. Designs often depicted scenes from Hindu mythology or regional legends, rendered through traditional block carving methods passed down through generations within artisan families. The craftsmanship involved careful application of natural dyes onto locally sourced silk or cotton.

The origin story blends influences; the names 'Jilbiyaar Kahe' (Goddess of Victory) and 'Kashidaar Dhadhiyan' (Fabrics for Worship) point to a fusion, possibly enhanced during Rajput patronage under local rulers like the Cheros. These prints gained recognition alongside other established traditions such as Varanasi silk.

It is important to note that while the name might seem familiar today due to later developments or similarities with other Indian fabrics, "Farrukhabad" itself doesn't originally signify a specific print style but rather refers back to Kashi textile heritage and its historical craft revival. The legacy lies in these visually rich patterns created on high-quality silk material using historical methods.
