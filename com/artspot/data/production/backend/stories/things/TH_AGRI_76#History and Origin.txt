
The historical roots of Sindhudurg and Ratnagiri lie deep within coastal western India, stretching back over millennia where both areas served as important maritime locations.

Evidence points towards significant pre-Islamic history for the Chaul (Sindur) and Dabok (Dabhol's historic part) portions, which were integral parts of ancient trading ports along the Konkan coastline. Their proximity to Goa was also notable historically during certain periods.

The arrival of the Portuguese in the early 16th century dramatically changed their trajectory for much of their history (until 1594). They utilized these locations as entrepôts and refuges, facilitating trade in pepper and chili seeds – "kokum" being an old term signifying chilies. The strategic Portuguese control contributed heavily during this era.

However, towards the latter part of the colonial period, especially after 1803 when Sindhudurg's Fort was captured by Angria Chola, and due to their inherent natural limitations for sustained pepper/chili cultivation compared to later inland Maharashtra districts with "kakdvip" or other favorable soils (and perhaps the name 'kokum' referring simply to chili locally), these regions' economic primacy shifted. They retained strategic importance in naval terms but transitioned from being prime agricultural zones for kokum.

The historical narrative of Sindhudurg and Ratnagiri is one of ancient trade, strategic conflict involving crucial Portuguese interactions, significant coastal encounters like the Dutch-Vijayanagar influence indirectly shaping their trajectory, evolving administrative boundaries impacting region classifications over centuries, and periods where other areas took precedence in cultivation or governance despite their foundational history.
