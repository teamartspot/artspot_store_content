
Bagru hand block printing has its roots in the heart of Rajasthan's Marwar region, primarily traced back to around three decades ago, likely commencing between roughly 1914 and 1935 (often cited as approximately 70 years). It isn't an ancient technique like some other Indian art forms but a distinct emergence compared to neighboring block printing traditions.

The foundational process involved intricate designs cut onto flat wooden blocks by expert crafts persons. These blocks were used multiple times using traditional techniques with colour paste mixed manually and applied directly over carved areas, sometimes needing resist blocking initially. However, this manual mixing proved challenging for achieving consistent tone across large runs of fabric, especially when printing lighter shades or fine lines repeatedly.

These hurdles naturally led to an evolution distinguishing Bagru from pure block print. Over time, artists transitioned towards using hand-cut stencil blocks (derived directly from the original wooden blocks). They slip-cast this intricate technique on sheets coated with colour paste, creating vibrant resist patterns, then pressed these stencils onto unstained fabrics laid beneath. This method ensured uniformity in application.

The shift likely occurred due to practical necessity and possibly as part of art education being taught through institutions like the Charkha centre dedicated to the craft. Stencil work simplified complex block carving tasks while enhancing consistency. Today, Bagru printing boasts rich earth tones, particularly browns on red base fabrics (cotton or silk remnants), creating visually compelling heritage textiles globally seen.
