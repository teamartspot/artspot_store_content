
Driftwood craft emerged from necessity in Japan's coastal regions long before becoming an esteemed art form. Ancient fishermen, reliant on the sea, found they needed tools and everyday objects while adrift or stranded. Salvaging wood washed ashore solved this problem; pieces were small, unique, and often imperfect yet perfectly usable for repairing nets, making floats, handles, and various essential implements requiring minimal shaping.

This practical tradition evolved naturally into artistry after World War II. Carvers observed how nature combined disparate parts – branches fused by fire or decay, bark adhered in weathering layers – to create surprisingly beautiful forms. This serendipity encouraged experimentation with arranging driftwood pieces together like a mosaic, creating larger sculptures where traditional wood carving wasn't feasible.

Driftwood craft became synonymous with finding value and aesthetics in neglected material. A distinctive technique involves preserving the natural grey state of the wood, avoiding bleaching or resin impregnation, to retain its history told through patina – lichens, barnacles, tool marks from shipwrecks centuries ago – allowing a sense of mystery, memory, age, and impermanence into each piece. The art emphasizes respecting nature's form rather than imposing its own structures upon the disparate driftwood parts, transforming what was seen as mere salvage through thoughtful arrangement into profound artistic expression.
