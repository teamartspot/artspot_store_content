
Kanjeevendu, known in the native telugu language as Kanjeeputa Kalam *or* Kalamkari itself (meaning thread craft), is a distinct form of traditional handcrafted silk painting originating specifically from Machilipatnam in East Godavari district of Andhra Pradesh. It stands separately from the more well-known Khajuraho Chitrapitham, also prevalent in Andhra.

Historically thought to have existed since Kali Yuga (ages ago), its significant flowering occurred during the Kakatiya dynasty rule over Machilipatnam. Today's revival is credited to two brothers—Rayanna Reddy and Allareddy Rayudu Mallikarjuna Rao—from this same region in Hyderabad's Deccan. The art involves intricate designs crafted on fine zari threads, gold wires (kundu), and sometimes plain silk fabric dyed beforehand.

Traditional themes included royal narratives, Hindu gods and goddesses (especially from the Bhagavata Purana), temple panels, floral borders, animal motifs, geometric patterns, and hunting scenes. The process of *ikat* (*kanjeeva puppu*) was fundamental right from the start – specific threads bound in bundles were dyed to create an ombre effect before weaving or warping the cloth.

Initially produced only for royalty until its 16th-century annexation by Hyderabad, it faced decline, prompting revivalists like Rayanna Reddy. It is now recognized as a UNESCƠ intangible cultural heritage. As one of India's oldest and living silk textile traditions in East South India, Machilipatnam Kanjeevendu continues its vibrant legacy today through talented artisans keeping the craft alive age-old techniques.
