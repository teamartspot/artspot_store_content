
Channapatna toys possess a rich history rooted in the small town of Channapatna in present-day Karnataka, near Bangalore. While famously associated with lacquerware boxes today, there are also traditional wooden dolls.

The legend most commonly linked to Channapatna involves Tipu Sultan, the ruler of Mysore from 1775-99. During his reign, Persian artisans were captured and forced to work at Bangarappa Square near Channapatna's palace grounds. When their employment lapsed, these skilled craftsmen took with them techniques for crafting lacquerware boxes, adapting designs known locally as "Kanakadas' toys." This marks the probable beginning of this enduring craft in the area around 180 years ago.

The British colonial era significantly shaped Channapatna craftsmanship. Their taste for intricate design and durable materials led to collaborations with native artisans like Srikanteshwara, Mysore's most renowned lacquer craftsman who worked near the town. Toys evolved from simple boxes into colourful miniature models: elephants, monkeys, cars like the Brighton Coach, and later aeroplanes gained prominence due to his inspiration.

Strict royal restrictions under Tipu forbade local artisans making things outside their assigned quarters, which further cemented Channapatna's reputation for lacquer crafts. The craft survived even when Mysore nobles were expelled after Tipu's fall; it thrived locally despite the shift away from royal patronage and Mysore itself becoming inactive by 1973.

Initially named "Patnagate" or Pattadakallu (before being anglicised to Channapatna), these lacquer crafts gained official recognition as India's first Geographical Indication. The distinctive bright, contrasting colours on black lacquer boxes remain instantly identifiable today.
