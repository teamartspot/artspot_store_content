
Aranḍachal Pradeś, India’s far-easternmost state known for its stunning natural landscapes and rich tribal cultures, houses some of Asia's oldest traditions in handmade carpet weaving. The origin of this craft is deeply intertwined with the beliefs and rituals of various indigenous tribes like those of Naga, Aborin, or Wangala.

Initially serving ritualistic purposes – used as wrappers during festivals to cover sacred objects dedicated to the gods by tribal shamans – these intricate felt pieces carry profound symbolic meanings related to deities. They are created from locally sourced animal fibres such as yak wool (from neighboring Arunachal regions) or hair, reflecting ancient shamanic artistry and symbolism.

With time, influenced by neighbouring cultures like Assam and Meghalaya, this unique tradition evolved into the exquisite handmade carpets we know today. The primary material now is hand-carded animal and sometimes vegetable fibres from specific areas: Naga Hills for fine wool, Pangan district for coconut coir ropes used in warp binding.

While retaining traditional motifs depicting deities, mythical creatures like elephants (Naga symbols), peacocks (associated with beauty or Aborin mythology), mountains, and villages on a geometric base, the craft focuses purely on hand-made felt pieces. These aren't pile carpets but intricate flat-weave wonders that have secured national recognition awards.

The craftsmanship involved meticulous knotting work in creating these dense felt structures deserves appreciation. Arunachal Pradesh's handmade carpet tradition stands as a testament to tribal ingenuity and artistic prowess preserved through generations, adapting perhaps for modern use while maintaining its core cultural identity.
