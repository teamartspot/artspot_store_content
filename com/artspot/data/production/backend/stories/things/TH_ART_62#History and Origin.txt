
Kasuti embroidery hails primarily from the Mysore region of Karnataka, India, flourishing largely between the mid-19th century and the early 20th century, during the latter part of the Wodeyar royal dynasty. Its lineage is often intertwined with the devadasi tradition – women consecrated to serve deities in temples dedicated to deities like Lakshmi and Saraswati through temple dance performances (kirtis). Some tales link Kasuti directly to court patronage following shifts from palace performances.

Characterized by its intricate gold thread work (zari) on a solid silk base, Kasuti stands distinct for its geometric precision. Motifs include lotuses, flowers rendered abstractly, paisley scrolls, medallions, and stylized animals, often executed with remarkable complexity and detail woven directly into the fabric rather than stitched later like other Indian embroidery styles.

While the direct devadasi connection to temple performances faded post-reforms, this art form endured. It integrated with the local textile industry, enriching fabrics used in courtly attire and eventually popularizing for general wear under regional kingships. Today, Kasuti remains a vital part of Mysore's cultural heritage, widely practiced by both professional weavers-artisans (kasudis) and dedicated hobbyists across South India, adorning sarees, dresses, and decorative fabrics primarily for special occasions like weddings.
