
Nestled within the picturesque landscape of Arunachal Pradesh's Lower Siang district, particularly involving the Wancho tribe along the banks of the tributaries to the Brahmaputra River, lies a significant origin point for one of Northeast India's cherished handicrafts – Wancho wooden craft. The name itself is deeply ingrained in its history; it's locally recognized as "Wancha Bhaj" or 'wood carving', directly naming the artisan community involved.

Initially part of daily life, Wancho wood carving dates back significantly longer than decorative art. Tribal artisans skilfully crafted essential tools and weapons for survival – robust axes (Kholom) used both agriculturally and in clearing forests, strong hoes (Bamong), sturdy spears (Yothung) vital to hunting needs, bows, arrows, and fishing implements like hooks, baskets, and containers. Carved from durable local woods such as Khair and Sal – sourced specifically from their forested surroundings including Khasia forests near Udanti village in Papum Pare district, which have historically supplied the Khair wood for Wancho carving.

The exceptional skill demonstrated by these artisans is evident not just in functional tools but also in beautiful ceremonial masks (Tutung). This craft represents more than material work; it embodies their cultural and spiritual heritage. Through government schemes aimed at preserving indigenous arts, including support provided to specific areas via PDS assistance cards, the Wancho tradition continues. It remains a vital part of not only their identity but as an integral element within Arunachal Pradesh's broader cultural mosaic, showcasing profound traditional craftsmanship utilizing locally sourced materials.
