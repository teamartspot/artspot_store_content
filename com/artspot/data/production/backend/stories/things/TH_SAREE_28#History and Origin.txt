
Kashmir, renowned for its exquisite handicrafts, is home to the Bagh Saree, originating from the village of Wakhra in the Kashmir Valley's Bagh region. This traditional handloom silk drape has a history rooted deeply within the weaver community since Mughal times.

The distinctive feature of a Bagh Saree lies not just in its colours but in the intricate *buti* or dotwork patterns that adorn it, often resembling peacocks, floral motifs, or abstract geometric designs. These fine dots are frequently larger centrally to create the illusion of higher quality zari (metallic thread), although historically, Bagh weaving did feature genuine gold and silver threads too.

Bagh Sarees traditionally employ rich, resplendent hues on a deep *kora* or base silk warp colour, typically black. Silk is usually the finest *shetar*, specifically raw silk sourced from Banaras (Bihar) for Mughal Emperor Akbar's court in Kashmir. A unique technique using wheat starch sizing (*safa lacini*) allows weavers to build intricate patterns through colour gradation without cutting threads.

The fabric structure is primarily a heavy *ganga-jamuna* or broken twill weave, resulting in its characteristic crispness and luster. Often paired with a *jamawar* border (zari), the flowing *pallu* at the end often features complex chintz motifs derived from intricate Mughlai miniature painting and Persian influence.

Over generations, Baggha families have maintained these specific weaving traditions, developing unique narrative compositions on their silks. Today, visiting weavers' clusters like those surrounding Lake Dalhousie in Srinagar offers a glimpse into this living heritage – the enduring elegance and storytelling craft of the Kashmiri silk saree.
