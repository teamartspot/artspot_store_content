
Kapadaganda: Dungaria Kondh is a unique and deeply cherished handicraft tradition from the remote village communities of Dungria and Kondha in the Bastar region of Odisha, India. This distinctive shawl art evolved among these isolated tribes who fiercely protected their way of life, including their language.

Initially, 'Kapadaganda' referred simply to a long strip-shaped cloth or piece of fabric used for weaving shawls, saris, and other garments. Over time, this term came specifically to denote the shawl itself from certain areas in Dungaria and parts of Kondha village. The motifs woven into these shawls are often abstract yet profoundly meaningful, frequently referencing local flora – the famous Sal tree (which inspired its colour name), large leaves (udanga paarvata), rain (varshini patra), deer skinning incidents (sakalikaranya), or significant tribal ceremonies like marriages.

Characterized by bold lines and geometric patterns rather than traditional figurative representation, Dungaria Kondh shawls are not created for immediate consumption but often laid down years in advance to symbolize hope. They serve vital purposes: marking the onset of winter (asravyartha), providing crucial warmth during night vigils (yagna kaapadanga) before weddings, keeping newborn infants warm ('bharimun kavadi'), and offering protection for pregnant women attending tribal dances. Made predominantly from silk or a silk-cotton blend using natural dyes, these shawls are more than just clothing items; they represent the ancient pride, endurance, isolation, and unwavering faith of the Dungria and Kondha communities in their unique artistic manifestation.
