
Known by its unique dark finish, Black Pottery refers to two distinct but equally fascinating traditions found in vastly different parts of the world: one from Korea and another deeply rooted among certain communities in Central & West Africa.

Often called On-yeon-jung or O-Paek, Korean black pottery stands out due to its signature *crackled glaze*. Achieved through careful layering of special iron-rich high-fired glazes over other delicate low-fired glazes, heating causes the upper layers to crack and form intricate, fine patterns against a deep charcoal-black surface. After cracking, pieces undergo further firing at lower temperatures until nightfall becomes a perfect uniform black.

This pottery transcends mere aesthetics; it's integral to Korean shamanistic rituals (e.g., *Hwa-eum* funerals for the deceased) due to its perceived spiritual power and its role in water purification filtration systems because impurities adhere to the internal ridges of the crackle glaze.

On the other side of the globe, African Black Pottery represents a different kind of tradition. Famous from Congo Zaire for its heavy pots (often called Nkisi Ntu or *nkishi*), these aren't glazed in the same way. Instead, their deep black comes naturally through specific mineral or organic pigments mixed with clay. The potter’s mark is sometimes embedded as a symbolic 'signature'.

These functional vessels served crucial purposes beyond beauty. They were used for storing water and food, but also held spiritual significance – acting as potent containers believed to influence community well-being (hence the nails protruding from them).

Though separated geographically, these are both traditions of Black Pottery deserving attention - intricate crafts born from specific cultural contexts across continents.
