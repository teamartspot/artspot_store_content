
Rajouri Chikri wood craft stands as a vibrant testament to Jammu & Kashmir's distinctive artistic heritage. Originating predominantly from the Rajouri region of Jammu, this intricate form utilizes teakwood blocks, expertly carved in bas-relief on all sides. Unlike other block printing traditions that utilize ink rolling stones or plane printing techniques, chikri artisans meticulously chip away excess wood with tools like mallets and chisels to reveal raised patterns.

The designs adorning these blocks are usually reserved for depicting divinities, deities,佛陀 Boddhisatvas from Buddhism, Hindu mythology, Persian court figures, and sometimes local folk heroes. These highly detailed carvings require immense skill and precision, ensuring the fine lines of faces, intricate jewelry, and ornate drapery remain clear.
Often polished with linseed oil or boiled linseed oil to glisten, the blocks become potent storytellers. A typical chikri block might weigh upwards of 80 kilograms due to its dense structure.

This traditional craft thrives primarily to fulfill religious and ceremonial requisites – a major market for mandaps (temple structures), diya holders (light lamps), *pani vasas* (water vessels placed near deities), door knockers, *kamandalus* (libation cups) dedicated to Shiva, intricately carved temple beams, lecterns (*mandap karkhanis*) with elaborate floral and fretwork designs.

The demand often comes from Vaishnavites using wood blocks for the Sri Krishnacaritra or Harivanshi Ramayan tradition specific to Garhwalers. In essence, Rajouri Chikri is a labor-intensive skill embodying reverence and craftsmanship dedicated to creating sacred objects unique to its origin place in Jammu region of India.*
