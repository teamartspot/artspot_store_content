
Paper Mache, known for vibrant colours and intricate designs applied primarily over terracotta bases in Kashmir, evolved as a response to local conditions and resources. While the craft's techniques share similarities with other South Asian traditions sometimes using pulp or egg white, its distinct narrative style and the specific use of tempera glazes developed within this region.

The origins of the art form are deeply intertwined with the lives of artisans from the Kashmir Valley, particularly regions like Srinagar. Initially serving a practical purpose during the challenging winter months when timber for boat repairs was scarce, artisans innovated by covering unfinished woodwork or even the hulls of small boats using a unique hand-painted paper mache. This created colourful, relatively durable coverings while allowing other materials to dry.

Over time, this decorative function matured, shifting focus entirely from craft utility to art. The aesthetic appeal and rich storytelling tradition associated with these paintings gained recognition. They began adorning household items like utensils, doors, and even musical instruments such as shehnais or nagaras, transforming interiors and adding cultural narratives through depictions of mythology, folklore, and scenes from daily life.

Today, Parchha is not just a craft but signifies the collective artistic expression and identity of Kashmir. Its creation involves mixing colours from local pigments with egg whites, gum arabic (or synthetic gums historically), and cow milk tempera, then applying multiple thin layers onto its core medium: terracotta or porous wood using specific knives (sharpi). These vibrant pictorial narratives serve as a unique window into Kashmir's profound history.
