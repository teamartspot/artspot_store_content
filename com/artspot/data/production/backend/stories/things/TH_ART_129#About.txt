
Warli painting represents one of India's oldest and most distinctive folk art traditions, primarily originating from the villages surrounding Pune in Maharashtra state. Executed traditionally as dry-paint on walls or occasionally wood panels using tempera and natural colours like red, yellow, and black clay pigments mixed with cow milk, its characteristic technique involves applying multiple translucent layers to build depth.

The art form doesn't use complex tools but simple implements held between the thumb and finger, allowing spontaneous gestural lines. These minimalistic strokes powerfully express deep spiritual concepts inherent in village life through symbolic representations rather than realistic depictions. A central theme is nature itself; often depicting trees as primordial sources (known as 'Vriksha Bandhu', or friend of the tree), crucial for survival.

Abstract geometric shapes like triangles and circles commonly symbolize humans, cattle, buildings, and other elements without intricate detail. Animals represent different village groups – villagers human are often stylized as dogs or peacocks. The compositions usually include representations of ancestral lineage or village deities, framed by a prominent tree figure connecting all elements.

Warli art is vibrant visual storytelling woven into the fabric of the community's identity over generations, using readily available materials and focusing on enduring motifs like nature, family, food, work, and the life cycle itself. It remains very much alive today as both decorative tradition within homes and an evolving form capturing contemporary relevance through practitioners while respecting its foundational themes.
