
Orissa Pattachitra, a vibrant and intricate style of traditional Indian painting, traces its origins back through centuries in the Eastern Indian state of Odisha. Initially developed as part of temple rituals, it began serving decorative purposes for deities during worship times – the initial term 'Patta' itself signifying an offering cloth used to clothe gods. This intimate connection with spiritual life meant these early paintings were devotional expressions filtered through visual form.

The distinctive narrative quality and specific iconography associated with Pattachitra art belong primarily to Odisha and temple traditions of South India, particularly evolving under the patronage of kings across various dynasties. The intricate detail required, especially for complex mythological scenes, likely demanded a well-established royal workshop environment initially, which encouraged artists' mastery.

Intricate brushwork defined its evolution, creating story-telling scrolls or panels on temple pillars and later becoming portable standalone works (referred to as 'Patta'). Over time, it evolved into the monumental form of Rupadeka. While initially confined to devotional contexts linked directly to royalty, its popularity grew independently until today. Modern Orissa Pattachitra artists still employ natural pigments from roots, minerals and leaves on silk fabric ('Pattu') or canvas ('Sthira' pattu), adding complex borders with characteristic scrolls (koshta) that frame the deity at the narrative center – a unique aspect of its visual language.
