
Kangra art stands as a vibrant regional style from North India's Himachal Pradesh region. Its origins trace back centuries, likely influenced by broader Indian artistic traditions including those from Tibet, Kashmir, and the Punjab.

The 17th century marked its true golden age, flourishing during Mughal invasions when Rajput rulers sought refuge in the inaccessible Kangra valley (located along the Beas River). This isolation allowed their distinct art style to develop, heavily inspired by Sanskrit religious literature. Paintings began appearing around this time on temple walls, manuscripts, palaces, and royal tombs.

This unique school is renowned for its secular themes, unlike much contemporary Mughal or Rajput art, even as it used Hindu subjects – depictions of divine love often involving Radha-Krishna were particularly popular alongside portraits of royalty and sant guru. The style blended indigenous techniques with influences from local artists like those from Pind Dadanpi.

Though its prominence faded after the 18th century, scholars have worked to preserve this art form through restoration efforts and conservation in recent times.
