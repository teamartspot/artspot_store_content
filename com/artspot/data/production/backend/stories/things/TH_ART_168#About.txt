
Varanasi's renowned heritage is significantly augmented by its centuries-old tradition of intricate wood carving. While famous for silk and brocade textiles woven with divine motifs, the artisans of Banaras have equally mastered the art of transforming diverse woods into exquisite decorative items.

Typically found on doors ('palki'), windows frames, panels from temple screens ('rajots'), and furniture – especially jharokhas (overhanging balconies) – this craftsmanship blends geometric patterns with flowing floral designs. Although the craft's visible evolution incorporated influences arriving with Mughal patronage during medieval times, its foundational roots lie deep in Hindu devotional aesthetics and regional folk traditions like Rajput art.

The most characteristic creations arise from religious commissions for temple panels and structures ('karkhanai shilpi'), alongside furniture, parrots perched on strings (ankor palak) – symbols of peace often adorning temples and homes. Jhumkas or jhoprayag are traditional ear-drop decorations fashioned by the renowned artists.

Artisans carve fine patterns from teak burl applewood, boxwood ('kaduk') or 'parsiya' variety, working these materials with remarkable patience to produce miniature details, sometimes so small that over a hundred intricate scenes fit onto a single plank measuring just 0.3 meters by 9 centimeters.

The creation process often involves hollowing out the wood and then painting it – possibly gilded – from within, allowing for decorative elements like pierced work on doors or windows to appear luminous against backgrounds crafted elsewhere.
