
Banaras Mural Painting, deeply intertwined with Varanasi's spiritual heritage since the 13th century during Akha Sthalapuri period, finds its earliest inspiration in temple motifs and epic narratives from Sanskrit texts like the Mahabharata. The Ajanta Caves' frescoes served as a primary model for early masters, shaping their understanding of composition, perspective (often stylized), proportion, and most importantly, color theory.

During the Mughal era, these paintings became even more intricate under Persian influence, adorning mansions and royal shrines with detailed scenes from Puranas, Bhagavata Leelas depicting Vaishnava traditions primarily through Radha-Krishna narratives, or diverse mythological storylines. However, after mid-20th century, many artists shifted focus towards painting contemporary houses due to changing patronage.

The technique involves applying vibrant colors onto a base of thread varnish layer by layer, creating relief effects and durability suited for temple architecture. Colors evolved from initial muted earth tones used near Ganga Ki Phool (Motiram) Bazaar in 1934, following Ajanta's chromatic style, to the rich jewel-tones characteristic of later periods. Banaras Mural Painting is not merely decorative; it serves as a vital visual narration medium connecting Vaishnava culture, spiritual beliefs, festival processions, and reflecting societal shifts through its surviving depictions on temple walls and other historical structures in Varanasi since 1934.
