
Rooted in the fertile coastal lands of present-day Odisha, Pattachitra is a vibrant form of devotional painting that has flourished for centuries under artisans known as *Sariga* or 'bamboo brush painters'. Emerging during the 15th century from temples and royal courts on the Coromandel Coast, these paintings primarily use bamboo skewers dipped in natural colour pigments to create intricate linear patterns, replacing entirely the conventional brush. This unique method yields a highly characteristic look.

Themes typically draw heavily from Hindu epics – Ramayana, Mahabharata, Puranas; Buddhist narratives or Vaishnavite devotional stories. Often executed on commissioned scrolls, Pattachitra initially served religious and historical purposes but now encompasses mythology, nature scenes, human portraits, and depictions of regional kings and queens (*Rajputras*), especially from the 18th century Madhubani-style courts. Today, it's not only practiced in its classical scroll form across several states inspired by Odisha, but also as decorative art on surfaces like cloth (banarsi dhoti and *Kantha*) and lacquerware.

Collections of original Pattachitra scrolls are rare due to their large size and historical fragility, yet museums house significant examples capturing the essence of this living tradition. The art is renowned for its fine detail, rhythmic composition, bold outlining, delicate washes, and unique palettes derived from minerals like ochre, malachite, or lapis lazuli ground with natural resins or gum arabic. It offers a rich window into India's devotional art history through bold narratives rendered in the distinctive stroke of the bamboo brush.
