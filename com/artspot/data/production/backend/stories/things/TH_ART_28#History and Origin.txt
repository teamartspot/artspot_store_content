
Dhokra, or Tsonwongwa, meaning 'to beat iron' in many Bantu languages across West Africa, refers to an ancient bronze-casting technique. Its origins trace back to prehistoric times around 600 BCE, emerging in the forested regions of what is now Ghana, Togo, Nigeria (particularly Benin), and surrounding areas like Burkina Faso and Niger.

Unlike methods relying solely on lost-wax duplication, which can be complex using clay or plaster, the Dhokra process utilized simpler, locally available materials. Crucial ingredients included natural resins, acids derived from plants like millet, sawdust for fuel (acting as a reducing agent in the smelting), and molten glass or tar obtained by heating sand.

The specific steps involved applying wax figures to a metal core using gum Arabic resin mixed with organic binders. This complex form was then encased in a fast-setting 'dough' made of fine mud, ash, sawdust, water, acid, copper sulfate, and plant sugars – much different from traditional lost-wax clay.

When fired locally (not directly imported), this composite mixture generated molten glass which effectively sealed the wax inside the mold. Submerged in a bath of copper-acid solution at low heat using bamboo blowpipes powered by bellows, artisans could selectively fuse metal particles onto the sand base over several days.

After achieving 'tinctorial bronze' – typically dark brown or black – the remaining acid, sawdust residue, and un fused parts were scraped out. The resulting piece was a unique form of terracotta or stoneware mosaic encased in solid copper, showcasing intricate detail achieved through this sophisticated pre-literate period metalworking system.
