
Early "miniatures" emerged from Byzantine traditions of creating *hyllage* or decorated capital letters for book illumination, a practice dating back to Late Antiquity. Initially employing Greek Uncial script on gold leaf backgrounds, these forms evolved after the Christianization and Carolingian Renaissance into intricate Latin illustrations within manuscripts.

The term roughly corresponds to European sacred manuscript painting from approximately 1000 AD through around 1450 AD. During this period, known as the Gothic style or International Gothic in its later peak, artists developed sophisticated methods using tempera paints on vellum or parchment. These highly detailed figures often incorporated gold grounds and vibrant colors derived primarily from lapis lazuli (used for ultramarine blue).

Miniatures served pivotal religious and didactic functions, common during Late Middle Ages when book illustration was waning elsewhere but flourishing in this format. They depicted key biblical scenes, saints, or events within cycles to illuminate texts like Psalters, Gospels, Lives of Saints, Song of Songs commentaries, and encyclopedias.

While primarily focusing on sacred subjects initially, leading figures sometimes included prominent lay patrons, reflecting the evolving purpose beyond purely spiritual representation. The technical mastery required for creating these tiny marvels placed significant pressure on illuminators to achieve remarkable detail despite their confined scale and complex compositions demanding careful composition work even at that small size. This tradition eventually faded after 1500 with the advent of printed books but left an indelible mark on European art history as some scholars now consider it distinct from the broader panel painting forms emerging during that time.
