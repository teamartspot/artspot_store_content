
The Nicobari mat, known locally as Chatrai or Hileuoi within the communities of the remote islands inhabited by the indigenous Nicobari people, represents centuries-old craftsmanship and cultural identity.

These mats are not merely functional items but hold deep significance in daily life and ceremonies. The knowledge of their intricate weaving techniques is passed down through generations of women among the Nicobari tributaries – including groups like the Satabdiya and the Yur community – particularly on islands like Uavela, Amini (Katchal), and Mihirapur (Oyster). Each mat tells a unique story woven with specific patterns signifying particular events or beliefs.

Originating from their traditional forest-based homes ('Dunga'), these Chatrai or Hileuoi are meticulously hand-woven primarily using coconut leaves ('Pal') and sometimes betel vine leaves ('Sindur'). Palm-fiber thread secured onto bamboo frames is the principal material, forming complex geometric designs unique to each lineage. Different motifs often represent distinct stages of life – from birth to marriage and beyond – or reflect beliefs about nature and ancestors.

Artisans skillfully transform raw palm fibers through aging, softening, and dyeing into fine threads before weaving them carefully onto temporary frames. This traditional method requires immense patience and expertise, as intricate patterns are built stitch by painstaking stitch using specialized hooking techniques.

Historically woven for practical purposes like sleeping or floor covering (from the name Chatrai), these mats evolved to represent social status, historical narratives, achievements, religious aspects, courtship rituals within the community, and even served protective roles during births. They stand as tangible links to Nicobari tribal oral traditions, beliefs, and history, preserved through their intricate patterns, embodying a unique visual language from these culturally rich islands of the Andaman & Nicobar archipelago.
