
Artists from Andhra Pradesh’s Madhav district created Mandana paintings as a form of folk art for temple decoration, though its presence has been historically noted across other parts of AP too. The name 'Mandana' itself translates from Sanskrit to mean 'gods', hinting perhaps at the divine subjects often depicted or the narrative style inherent within these works.

Characterised by striking elongations – notably very long heads and arms – Mandana figures possess distinct proportions setting them apart from other Indian art traditions. Figures are typically thin, wearing form-fitting dhotis or skirts and often carrying water pots slung over one shoulder. Their iconic status is further enhanced when adorned with specific symbols, particularly the *kankaya sironiki* (traditional necklace adornment made of five strings representing sacred thread), headgear such as the *gottuga chinna pagada*, a short scarf-like piece worn under the *pagada,* or holding specific implements like *padakonda gandasastra* associated with temple deities.

Mandana artists traditionally used natural colours mixed from locally available materials. The base was usually lime paste applied over newly plastered brick temple walls (dok-dhur murthi), or white on a dark, organic background like that prepared for *Kalamkali*. This art form, deeply connected to regional mythology and devotional practices, is celebrated as the people’s painting with its unique visual vocabulary. However, its use in contemporary contexts faces challenges as traditions evolve; vibrant yet often fleeting murals are created, reflecting a rich cultural heritage intertwined with religious expression but also indicating some struggle for preservation against changing times.
